SEC 1.0

Scope

▼
Standard Purpose & Application: API RP 571 provides guidance to inspection and materials personnel on identification, causes, susceptibility, morphology, and inspection techniques for damage mechanisms affecting fixed equipment in refining and petrochemical industries.
Section Description
Purpose Discusses damage mechanisms in refining, petrochemical, and other industrial applications. Acts as a reference for pressure equipment integrity.
Key Applications Identification of damage, inspection strategies, IOWs, CCDs, RBI, FFS assessments, incident investigations, and NDE techniques.
Standard Format Name, Description, Affected Materials, Critical Factors, Affected Units/Equipment, Appearance/Morphology, Prevention/Mitigation, Inspection/Monitoring, Related Mechanisms, References.

3.1

885 °F (475 °C) Embrittlement

Metallurgical Degradation
▼
Description of Damage: A loss of ductility and fracture toughness in stainless steels containing a ferrite phase due to high-temperature exposure.
Damage Type 885 °F (475 °C) Embrittlement
Affected Materials
  • 400 series SS (e.g., 405, 409, 410)
  • Duplex stainless steels
  • Austenitic (300 series) SS weld metals with ferrite
Main Cause Exposure to temperatures between 600 °F and 1000 °F (315 °C to 540 °C), causing the formation of an embrittling metallic phase.
Critical Factors
  • Alloy composition (higher chromium and ferrite increase susceptibility)
  • Operating temperature and time exposed
  • Cooling rate after welding
Locations
  • Fractionator trays and internals in high-temperature vessels (crude, vacuum, FCC, and coker units)
  • Duplex stainless steel heat exchanger tubes
Appearance
  • Cracking (often discovered during maintenance, start-up, or shutdown)
  • Increase in hardness
  • Fails bend or impact testing
Prevention
  • Avoid exposing susceptible materials to the 600 °F to 1000 °F range
  • Use non-susceptible materials
  • Control temperatures during start-up and shutdown
  • Heat treatment followed by rapid cooling can reverse it
Inspection
  • Impact or bend testing of a removed sample (most effective)
  • Visual inspection for cracks on trays and hardware
  • Field hardness or hammer testing (with caution)
Related Sigma phase embrittlement (3.56).

3.2

Amine Corrosion

Uniform / Localized Thinning
▼
Description of Damage: Localized corrosion of carbon steel in amine treating processes, driven by acid gases and amine degradation products.
Damage Type Amine Corrosion
Affected Materials
  • Primarily carbon steel
  • 300 series SS and other stainless steels are generally resistant
Main Cause Dissolved acid gases (CO2, H2S), heat stable amine salts, degradation products, and oxygen leaks in amine systems.
Critical Factors
  • Unit design and operating practices
  • Type of amine (MEA is most aggressive, MDEA is least)
  • High temperatures and process stream velocities
  • Accumulation of heat stable salts and oxygen in-leakage
Locations
  • Amine units removing H2S, CO2, and mercaptans
  • Regenerator reboiler, regenerator, and associated piping
  • Areas of high temperature, high velocity, or turbulence
Appearance
  • Uniform thinning in localized areas or under-deposit attack
  • Greater metal loss at high velocity or turbulent locations
  • Preferential attack on welds
Prevention
  • Proper system operation, controlling temperature and acid gas levels
  • Avoid oxygen in-leakage and buildup of heat stable salts
  • Use corrosion-resistant alloys in high-velocity or high-temperature areas
  • Remove solids by filtration and consider corrosion inhibitors
Inspection
  • Visual inspection of internal surfaces (flow impingement, turbulent areas, welds)
  • Ultrasonic testing (UT) and profile radiographic testing (RT) for thin regions
  • Corrosion monitoring with probes, coupons, and solution analysis
Related Amine SCC (3.3).

Amine Stress Corrosion Cracking

Environmental Cracking
▼
Description of Damage: Cracking of steels caused by the combined action of tensile stress and aqueous alkanolamine solutions used to absorb acid gases.
Damage Type Amine Stress Corrosion Cracking (SCC)
Affected Materials Carbon steel and low-alloy steels.
Main Cause High residual tensile stresses (from welding or cold working) combined with exposure to amine solutions.
Critical Factors
  • High residual stress levels from un-stress-relieved welds
  • Type of amine (MEA and DEA are more likely to cause cracking)
  • Increased temperatures increase cracking risk, but it can occur at ambient temperatures
Locations
  • Non-postweld-heat-treated (non-PWHT) carbon steel piping and equipment in amine service
  • Contactors, absorbers, strippers, regenerators, and heat exchangers
  • Primarily at or adjacent to welds
Appearance
  • Surface-breaking cracks, often parallel to welds or fanning out from nozzles
  • Usually found in the high residual stress zone near the weld
  • Cracks are typically intergranular, oxide-filled, and may have multiple branches
Prevention
  • Postweld heat treatment (PWHT) for all carbon steel welds (minimum 1175 °F)
  • Use solid or clad stainless steel or corrosion-resistant alloys
  • Thoroughly water wash non-PWHT equipment before welding or steam out
Inspection
  • Wet fluorescent magnetic particle testing (WFMT), eddy current, or ACFM for surface cracks
  • Angle beam ultrasonic testing (SWUT or PAUT) to detect and size cracks
  • Acoustic emission testing (AET) to locate cracks
Related Caustic SCC (3.15), Carbonate SCC (3.12), Ammonia SCC (3.4).

Ammonia Stress Corrosion Cracking

Environmental Cracking
▼
Description of Damage: Cracking that occurs in susceptible alloys under tensile stress when exposed to aqueous ammonia or anhydrous ammonia environments.
Damage Type Ammonia Stress Corrosion Cracking (SCC)
Affected Materials
  • Copper-zinc alloys (brasses with >15% zinc) in aqueous ammonia
  • Carbon steel (especially high-strength) in anhydrous ammonia
Main Cause Combination of residual stress and exposure to ammonia or ammoniacal compounds, often requiring traces of oxygen.
Critical Factors
  • Copper alloys: Zinc content, presence of water and oxygen, pH > 8.5
  • Carbon steel: Anhydrous ammonia (<0.2% water), lack of stress relief, oxygen contamination
  • High residual stresses from fabrication or welding
Locations
  • Copper alloy tubes in heat exchangers where ammonia is a contaminant or neutralizer
  • Non-stress-relieved carbon steel tanks, piping, and equipment in ammonia refrigeration or storage
Appearance
  • Copper alloys: Surface cracks with bluish corrosion products, transgranular or intergranular
  • Carbon steel: Intergranular cracking at exposed, non-stress-relieved welds and heat-affected zones (HAZs)
Prevention
  • Copper alloys: Use alloys with <15% zinc, cupro-nickels, or 300 series SS
  • Carbon steel: Add at least 0.2% water to ammonia, perform stress relief on welds, use low-strength steel, and prevent oxygen ingress
Inspection
  • Monitor pH and ammonia content in water
  • Copper alloys: Eddy current or visual testing for exchanger tubes
  • Carbon steel: WFMT, angle beam UT (SWUT/PAUT), or acoustic emission testing for welds and HAZs
Related None.

Ammonium Bisulfide Corrosion

Uniform / Localized Thinning
▼
Description of Damage: Aggressive corrosion occurring in units handling alkaline sour water, particularly in areas of high turbulence and high ammonium bisulfide concentration.
Damage Type Ammonium Bisulfide Corrosion (Alkaline Sour Water)
Affected Materials
  • Carbon steel and low-alloy steels
  • Admiralty brass and copper alloys are rapidly corroded
  • 300 series SS, duplex SS, nickel alloys, and titanium are more resistant
Main Cause Precipitation and hydration of ammonium bisulfide (NH4HS) salts in reactor effluent and sour water streams.
Critical Factors
  • NH4HS concentration (>2 wt% is increasingly corrosive for carbon steel)
  • High fluid velocity and localized turbulence
  • Presence of cyanides or oxygen, which can destroy protective sulfide films
Locations
  • Hydroprocessing reactor effluent systems (air coolers, separators, sour water draw piping)
  • FCC and coker fractionator overheads
  • Sour water stripper overheads and amine unit regenerators
Appearance
  • General thickness loss with extremely high localized wall loss in turbulent areas
  • Localized under-deposit corrosion at low velocities
  • Fouling and plugging of heat exchangers
Prevention
  • Proper design for balanced flow and low velocities
  • Use corrosion-resistant alloys (e.g., duplex SS, Alloy 825) for high velocities
  • Inject low-oxygen wash water to dilute and dissolve salts
Inspection
  • UT and RT scanning focused on turbulent areas and changes in flow direction
  • Inspect water injection points and spray nozzles
  • Electromagnetic techniques (IRIS, MFL, ECT) for exchanger tubes
Related Erosion-corrosion (3.27), ammonium chloride corrosion (3.6), concentration cell corrosion (3.19), titanium hydriding (3.66), chloride SCC (3.17).

Ammonium Chloride and Amine Hydrochloride Corrosion

Uniform / Localized Thinning
▼
Description of Damage: Highly localized corrosion, often pitting, occurring underneath deposited ammonium chloride or amine salts, even without a free water phase.
Damage Type Ammonium Chloride and Amine Hydrochloride Corrosion
Affected Materials All commonly used materials. Carbon and low-alloy steels are least resistant; titanium and nickel alloys (625, C276) are most resistant.
Main Cause Precipitation of hygroscopic ammonium chloride or amine salts that absorb tiny amounts of water, creating a highly concentrated, acidic, and corrosive environment.
Critical Factors
  • Concentrations of NH3, HCl, and amine salts
  • Temperature (salts can precipitate up to 400 °F / 205 °C, above the water dew point)
  • Availability of small amounts of water, which triggers aggressive corrosion
Locations
  • Crude tower overheads, top trays, and top pumparounds
  • Hydroprocessing reactor effluent streams
  • Catalytic reforming H2 recycle systems
  • FCC and coker fractionator overheads
Appearance
  • Whitish, greenish, or brownish salt deposits (which may wash away during steam out)
  • Highly localized pitting and corrosion underneath the deposits
Prevention
  • Limit chlorides in feeds (desalting, caustic addition, treating make-up hydrogen)
  • Use continuous or intermittent water washing to dissolve and flush salts
  • Monitor temperatures to operate above the salt deposition point
Inspection
  • RT or UT scanning (AUT) to detect highly localized thinning
  • Monitor water injection facilities and pressure drops (which indicate fouling)
  • Use electromagnetic techniques for exchanger tubes
Related Hydrochloric acid corrosion (3.37), Chloride SCC (3.17), Aqueous organic acid corrosion (3.7), Concentration cell corrosion (3.19).
3.7

Aqueous Organic Acid Corrosion

Uniform / Localized Thinning
▼
Description of Damage: Corrosion caused by low molecular weight organic acids (like formic or acetic acid) that form from thermal decomposition of crude oil compounds or additives, condensing in water phases.
Damage Type Aqueous Organic Acid Corrosion
Affected Materials
  • Carbon steel and low-alloy steels
  • Most corrosion-resistant alloys (e.g., austenitic SS) are generally resistant (unless halogens are present)
Main Cause Dissolution of volatile, light organic acids into the water phase, leading to reduced pH and acidic corrosion.
Critical Factors
  • Type and quantity of organic acids (formic and acetic are most corrosive)
  • System pH, fluid velocity, and temperature
  • Processing of higher Total Acid Number (TAN) crudes or use of certain oilfield additives
Locations
  • Crude, vacuum, visbreaker, and coker fractionator overhead systems
  • Areas where water accumulates (separator drum boots, tray liquid hold-ups)
  • High-velocity or turbulent areas (elbows, tees, control valves)
Appearance
  • General and localized wall thinning
  • Often leaves surfaces smoothly grooved in flowing areas
  • Localized pitting in low-velocity or condensing areas
Prevention
  • Inject acid-neutralizing additives, adjusted based on crude TAN and water sample analysis
  • Use filming amines (though less effective than neutralizers)
  • Upgrade to corrosion-resistant alloys
Inspection
  • UT and RT techniques for metal loss assessment
  • Monitor water pH, especially in overhead accumulation drums
  • Infrared thermography to detect liquid water locations
Related HCl corrosion (3.37), Naphthenic acid corrosion (3.46).

Atmospheric Corrosion

Uniform / Localized Thinning
▼
Description of Damage: Corrosion of exposed metals caused by atmospheric moisture, worsened by marine environments, industrial pollution, and airborne contaminants.
Damage Type Atmospheric Corrosion
Affected Materials Carbon steel, low-alloy steels, and copper-alloyed aluminum.
Main Cause Moisture from humidity, rainfall, or cooling tower mist contacting unprotected metal surfaces.
Critical Factors
  • Physical location (marine and industrial environments are worst)
  • Amount of moisture and presence of airborne contaminants (salts, sulfur, chlorides)
  • Temperatures below 250 °F (120 °C); above this, surfaces are usually dry
  • Designs that trap water
Locations
  • Unpainted or uninsulated carbon steel piping and equipment operating below 250 °F
  • Equipment with deteriorated paint/coatings
  • Piping resting on supports where water gets trapped
  • Piers, docks, and equipment downwind of cooling towers
Appearance
  • General or localized metal loss
  • Distinctive iron oxide (red rust) scaling
  • Localized corrosion where coatings have failed
Prevention
  • Proper surface preparation and application of paints or coatings
  • Avoid designs that trap water
  • Proper mothballing of idled equipment
Inspection
  • Visual testing (VT) for rust, coating damage, and trapped moisture
  • UT (straight/angle beam, EMAT, GWT) and RT to measure remaining wall thickness
  • Pulsed eddy current (PEC) for screening
Related Corrosion under insulation (3.22), Concentration cell corrosion (3.19).

Boiler Water and Steam Condensate Corrosion

Uniform / Localized Thinning
▼
Description of Damage: General corrosion, pitting, or flow-accelerated corrosion (FAC) in boiler and condensate systems, primarily driven by dissolved oxygen, carbon dioxide, or flow disturbances.
Damage Type Boiler Water and Steam Condensate Corrosion
Affected Materials Carbon steel and low-alloy steels.
Main Cause Dissolved oxygen causing oxygen pitting, dissolved carbon dioxide causing carbonic acid corrosion, or high velocity/turbulence preventing protective magnetite scale formation (FAC).
Critical Factors
  • Concentration of dissolved gases (O2 and CO2)
  • Water pH, temperature, and quality of feedwater
  • Performance of deaeration and oxygen scavenging treatments
  • Flow disturbances causing FAC
Locations
  • Deaerating equipment, feedwater lines, and pumps
  • Stage heaters, economizers, and boiler tubes
  • Condensate return systems and process unit reboilers
  • Areas downstream of flow disturbances (elbows, orifices, reducers)
Appearance
  • Oxygen corrosion: Deep pitting, especially where water temperature rises rapidly
  • Carbon dioxide corrosion: Smooth thinning or grooving of pipe walls
  • FAC: Wall thinning with a surface free of oxide scale, often with a specific flow pattern
Prevention
  • Mechanical deaeration and chemical oxygen scavenging (e.g., sulfite, hydrazine)
  • Amine inhibitor treatments for condensate systems
  • Proper boiler blowdown and venting
  • For FAC, adjust pH, control oxygen levels carefully, or upgrade to Cr-Mo steel
Inspection
  • Monitor water parameters: pH, dissolved gases, iron, copper, conductivity
  • Vacuum testing for air leaks
  • UT and RT to monitor pipe wall thinning
  • Offline inspection of boiler tubes
Related CO2 corrosion (3.18), Corrosion fatigue (3.21), Erosion-corrosion (3.27), Oxygenated water corrosion (3.49), Ammonia SCC of copper alloys (3.4).

3.10

Brine Corrosion

Uniform / Localized Thinning
▼
Description of Damage: Widespread or localized pitting corrosion on equipment exposed to aqueous solutions of dissolved salts (typically chlorides).
Damage Type Brine Corrosion
Affected Materials
  • Carbon steel, alloy steel, and aluminum are highly susceptible
  • Stainless steels can suffer chloride pitting/crevice corrosion
  • Copper and Ni-Cr-Mo alloys have better resistance
  • FRP (fiberglass) is immune
Main Cause Breakdown of protective passive layers by chlorides, leading to an autocatalytic acidic pitting process.
Critical Factors
  • Salt concentration, oxygen levels, and temperature (higher increases corrosion)
  • Low pH increases aggressiveness and decreases passive layer stability
  • Presence of free chlorine (biocides) or H2S accelerates attack
  • Pitting Resistance Equivalent Number (PREN) for stainless steels
Locations
  • Crude unit desalters and effluent systems
  • Effluent water treatment piping, ponds, and separators
  • Seawater (firewater) systems and water softeners
Appearance
  • Carbon steel: Heavy scaling, tubercles, under-deposit corrosion, wide pits
  • Stainless steel: Sharp, deep, isolated pits
  • Can show preferential corrosion in welds/HAZs for carbon steel
Prevention
  • Eliminate dissolved oxygen (vacuum deaeration or chemical scavenging)
  • Maintain low residual chlorine levels
  • Upgrade to higher PREN alloys (Ni-Cr-Mo) or non-metallics (FRP, PVC)
  • Apply and maintain internal coatings (epoxy)
Inspection
  • UT and RT for external thickness measurements
  • GWT for screening long pipe runs
  • Monitor dissolved oxygen and use corrosion coupons/probes

Mechanical Degradation
▼
Description of Damage: Sudden rapid fracture under stress (residual or applied) with little to no evidence of ductility or plastic deformation.
Damage Type Brittle Fracture
Affected Materials Carbon steels and low-alloy steels (especially older ones), and 400 series SS. Materials susceptible to other embrittling mechanisms (sigma phase, temper, strain-aging, titanium hydriding).
Main Cause The combination of a susceptible material, a flaw or stress concentrator, and high enough residual or applied stress, usually at low temperatures.
Critical Factors
  • Material's fracture toughness (resistance to crack-like flaws).
  • Size, shape, and stress concentration effect of the flaw.
  • Amount of residual and applied stress.
  • Presence of embrittling phases, impurities (S, P), and large grain size.
  • Thicker material sections have inherently lower resistance.
  • Occurs at temperatures below the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature.

Prevention
  • Use materials compliant with ASME BPVC low-temperature toughness requirements.
  • Control operating conditions (pressure, temperature) to minimize stress at low temperatures.
  • Perform engineering evaluations (API 579) for existing equipment.
  • Select materials with good low-temperature toughness (e.g., nickel-alloyed steel, 300 series SS).
Inspection Routine inspection is not normally used to detect brittle fracture itself, but susceptible vessels can be inspected for pre-existing flaws using Magnetic Particle Testing (MT), Penetrant Testing (PT), or Ultrasonic Testing (UT).

Carburization

Metallurgical Degradation
▼
Description of Damage: The absorption of carbon into a material at elevated temperatures in a carbonaceous environment, making the steel hard, brittle, and prone to cracking or spalling.
Damage Type Carburization
Affected Materials Carbon steel, low-alloy steels, 300 and 400 series SS, cast stainless steels, nickel-based alloys with high iron content, and HK/HP alloys.
Main Cause Exposure to a high carbon activity gas phase (hydrocarbons, coke, CO, methane) and low oxygen potential at temperatures typically above 1100 °F (595 °C).

  • Temperature high enough for carbon diffusion.

  • Reduce the carbon activity of the process.
  • Add low levels of reactive sulfur to the process stream to prevent carbon absorption.
Inspection
  • Destructive sampling for chemical/physical testing or metallography.
  • Hardness testing and field metallography for early stages.
  • Measurement of magnetic permeability (ferromagnetism) can monitor extent of carburization.
  • Advanced ultrasonic testing (TOFD) for case depth. Do not hammer test heavily carburized tubes!

▼
Description of Damage: Localized corrosion due to the concentration of caustic solutions (NaOH, KOH) or corrosive salts, usually occurring under evaporative or high heat transfer conditions.
Damage Type Caustic Corrosion (Caustic Gouging)
Affected Materials Primarily carbon steel, low-alloy steels, and 400 series SS. 300 series SS is generally resistant up to 160 °F–210 °F.

  • Presence of caustic (e.g., from water treatment, acid neutralization, leaks).

  • Proper design of steam-generating equipment (ensure adequate water flow, minimize hot spots, reduce free caustic).
  • Proper mixing and dilution at process caustic injection points.
  • Upgrade to Alloy 400 or other nickel-based alloys for high-strength caustic at elevated temperatures.
Inspection
  • Ultrasonic Testing (UT) for general and localized thickness loss.
  • Radiographic Testing (RT).
  • Permanently mounted thickness sensors.
  • Visual inspection (boroscope) for internal access.

Cavitation

Mechanical Degradation
▼
Description of Damage: A form of wear caused by the formation and instantaneous collapse of tiny vapor bubbles in a liquid, exerting severe localized impact forces that cause metal loss.
Damage Type Cavitation
Affected Materials Most common materials (copper, brass, cast iron, carbon steel, low-alloy steels, stainless steels, nickel alloys).
Main Cause Absolute pressure of the liquid falling below its vapor pressure, leading to the formation and rapid collapse of vapor bubbles.
Critical Factors
  • Inadequate Net Positive Suction Head (NPSH) in pumps.
  • Temperatures approaching the boiling point of the liquid.
  • Accelerated by the presence of solid/abrasive particles or corrosive environments.

  • Increase pump suction pressure.
  • Use hard surfacing or more corrosion-resistant materials, though material changes alone may not fully solve the mechanical issue.
Inspection
  • Visual Testing (VT) of suspect areas (e.g., using a boroscope during shutdowns).
  • Ultrasonic Testing (UT) to measure remaining thickness if the damaged area is large enough.
  • Radiographic Testing (RT) to quantify thickness loss.
  • Acoustic monitoring to detect characteristic cavitation sound frequencies.

Chloride Stress Corrosion Cracking

Environmental Cracking
▼
Description of Damage: Surface-initiated cracking caused by the combined action of tensile stress, temperature, and an aqueous chloride environment.
Damage Type Chloride Stress Corrosion Cracking (Cl- SCC)
Affected Materials 300 series SS (highly susceptible) and some nickel-based alloys. Duplex stainless steels are more resistant. Carbon and low-alloy steels are NOT susceptible.
Main Cause Presence of inorganic chloride ions in water, combined with tensile stress and elevated temperatures.
Critical Factors
  • Chloride concentration (no practical lower limit due to concentrating mechanisms).
  • Temperatures typically above 140 °F (60 °C).
  • Low pH and presence of dissolved oxygen accelerate cracking.
  • Tensile stress (usually residual from welding or cold working).
  • Nickel content (greatest susceptibility at 8% to 12% Ni).

Prevention
  • Use materials resistant to Cl- SCC (e.g., carbon steels, higher-nickel alloys).
  • Use low-chloride water for hydrotesting and dry quickly.
  • Apply suitable coatings under insulation.
  • Avoid designs that create stagnant regions where chlorides concentrate.
Inspection
  • Visual Testing (VT) for advanced stages.
  • Penetrant Testing (PT) (may require special surface preparation for tight cracks).
  • Eddy Current Testing (ECT) for condenser tubes.
  • Angle beam UT (SWUT/PAUT) can be used but is difficult due to branching cracks.

  • Visual Testing (VT), Ultrasonic Testing (UT), and Radiographic Testing (RT) for thickness loss.

Concentration Cell Corrosion

Uniform / Localized Thinning
▼
Description of Damage: Localized corrosion driven by an electrochemical cell created by differences in the environment (often oxygen concentration) between an occluded area (like under a deposit or in a crevice) and the surrounding bulk environment.
Damage Type Concentration Cell Corrosion
Affected Materials Materials whose overall aqueous corrosion resistance correlates to susceptibility. Carbon steel is most susceptible. Low-alloy and stainless steels are also susceptible depending on the alloy and environment.
Main Cause A differential in chemical species (typically oxygen) between an occluded cell and the surrounding fluid, creating a galvanic couple where the occluded (low-oxygen) area becomes the anode and corrodes faster.
Critical Factors
  • Presence of an aqueous environment.
  • An occluded cell (e.g., under deposits, crevices, pipe contact points, soil/air interfaces).
  • Oxygen concentration differential (or other species like sulfur/chlorides in air-free environments).
  • Accelerates existing corrosion rates rather than creating a new corrosion mechanism.
Locations Anywhere deposits (sludge, scale, dirt) build up inside or outside equipment. Crevices at bolted joints, flange faces, baffle holes, peeling coatings, and where pipes rest on supports.
Appearance Unevenly corroded surface, often described as pitting. In austenitic stainless steels with chlorides, it appears as sharp, narrow, deep pits.
Prevention
  • Prevent dirt and deposits from building up against or beneath equipment.
  • Minimize deposit/scale formation in operating units.
  • Design pipe supports to avoid trapping dirt and moisture (e.g., fully welded contact points).
  • Maintain protective coatings.
  • Upgrade materials where practical.
Inspection
  • Visual Testing (VT) with pit gaging.
  • Ultrasonic Testing (UT) from the opposite side to measure remaining wall thickness.
  • UT scanning (AUT, close-grid) or Guided Wave Testing (GWT) for large surfaces.
  • Monitor heat exchanger thermal performance (pressure drop increases indicating fouling).

Affected Materials Carbon steel, stainless steels, copper alloys, aluminum alloys, titanium, and nickel alloys.
Main Cause Corrosive properties of cooling water (fresh, brackish, salt) combined with fouling, scaling, and microbiological growth.

  • Cooling water temperature (higher temperatures increase corrosion/scaling).

  • Keep process-side inlet temperatures of water-cooled exchangers below 140 °F (60 °C).
  • Maintain optimal water velocities to prevent fouling and erosion.
  • Perform periodic mechanical cleaning.
  • Keep cooling water on the tube side to minimize stagnant areas.
Inspection
  • Monitor water chemistry (pH, oxygen, biological activity, scaling potential).
  • Monitor heat exchanger performance (U-factors).
  • Use continuous corrosion monitoring devices (coupons, ER probes).

Corrosion Fatigue

Mechanical Degradation
▼
Description of Damage: A form of fatigue cracking in which cracks develop under the combined effects of cyclic loading and a corrosive environment.
Damage Type Corrosion Fatigue
Affected Materials All metals and alloys.
Main Cause Combined effects of cyclic stress (mechanical or thermal) and corrosion, which causes failure at lower stress levels and fewer cycles than purely mechanical fatigue.
Critical Factors
  • Cyclic stress level and frequency.
  • Stress concentrators like pits or notches.
  • Corrosivity of the environment.
  • Unlike pure mechanical fatigue, there is no endurance limit in corrosion fatigue.
Locations Rotating equipment (pump shafts), deaerator welds, cycling boiler buckstay attachments, and any equipment subjected to cyclic stress in a corrosive environment.
Appearance
  • Cracks are brittle, straight, transgranular, and unbranched.
  • Often initiate at corrosion pits and may be filled with corrosion scale.
  • In cycling boilers, cracks can appear bulbous with multiple lobes ("rabbit ears").
Prevention
  • Select corrosion-resistant materials or apply coatings/inhibitors.
  • Minimize stress concentrators by grinding welds smooth.
  • Use Post-Weld Heat Treatment (PWHT) to reduce residual stresses.
  • Control water chemistry and start up boilers slowly.
Inspection
  • Focus on stress concentrators and welds.
  • Use Visual Testing (VT), Penetrant Testing (PT), Magnetic Particle Testing (MT), and Wet Fluorescent Magnetic Particle Testing (WFMT).
  • Ultrasonic Testing (UT) methods (SWUT, PAUT) and Alternating Current Field Measurement (ACFM) are also useful.
  • Note: Cracks can be very tight and difficult to detect when filled with corrosion products.

Uniform / Localized Thinning
▼
Description of Damage: Corrosion of piping, pressure vessels, and structural components resulting from water trapped under insulation or fireproofing.
Damage Type Corrosion Under Insulation (CUI)
Affected Materials Carbon steel, low-alloy steels, 300 series and 400 series stainless steels (SS), and duplex SS.
Main Cause Prolonged wetting from moisture or water trapped underneath the insulation material.
Critical Factors
  • Temperature: Most severe between 10°F and 350°F (-12°C to 175°C) for carbon steel; 140°F to 350°F (60°C to 175°C) for 300 series SS.
  • Duration of wetting and the insulation's ability to retain or wick water.
  • Poor insulation design or maintenance that allows water ingress.
  • Contaminants like chlorides or sulfur compounds from the environment or leached from the insulation.
Locations
  • Areas with damaged insulation, vapor barriers, or weatherproofing.
  • Insulation termination points, such as flanges, vertical pipe bottoms, and pipe supports.
  • Dead-legs, low points, and leaking steam tracing.
  • Equipment exposed to cooling tower drift, deluge systems, or frequent rain.

  • Carbon/low-alloy steel: Localized, rough, uneven, and pitted corrosion covered with a loose, flaky scale.
  • Stainless steels: Pitting, crevice corrosion, and Chloride Stress Corrosion Cracking (SCC).
  • External signs include damaged jacketing, bulges (from corrosion products), rust stains, and missing bands.
Prevention
  • Apply high-quality, immersion-resistant coatings (e.g., flame-sprayed aluminum) before insulating.
  • Maintain insulation, jacketing, and sealants to prevent water ingress.
  • Use non-wicking or low-chloride insulation materials.
  • Wrap stainless steel in thin aluminum foil to provide a galvanic barrier against SCC.
  • Remove unnecessary insulation where heat conservation is not required.
Inspection
  • Start with a systematic inspection plan based on equipment history and visual external signs.
  • The most effective method is completely removing insulation to inspect via VT, UT, or pit gages.
  • Non-invasive screening methods include Guided Wave Testing (GWT), Radiography (RT), Pulsed Eddy Current (PEC), and infrared thermography.

Creep and Stress Rupture

Metallurgical Degradation
▼
Description of Damage: A time-dependent, continuous deformation of stressed metal components at high temperatures, which occurs even below the material's elastic yield stress and eventually leads to cracking and rupture.
Damage Type Creep and Stress Rupture
Affected Materials All metals and alloys, when exposed to temperatures above their specific high-temperature thresholds.
Main Cause Prolonged exposure to stress at high temperatures (typically greater than half of the material's absolute melting temperature).
Critical Factors
  • Material composition, applied stress, and temperature.
  • Highly sensitive to small increases: a 25°F (15°C) temp increase or 15% stress increase can cut the component's remaining life in half.
  • Coarse-grained materials generally resist creep better than fine-grained materials.
  • Low creep ductility can cause sudden cracking with little visible deformation.
Locations
  • Fired heater tubes, tube supports, and furnace internals.
  • Hot-wall catalytic reforming reactors, FCC reactors/regenerators, and high-pressure steam boiler tubes.
  • Weld Heat-Affected Zones (HAZ) in high-stress areas or at dissimilar metal welds.
Appearance
  • Initial stages: Micro-voids form at grain boundaries (only visible under an electron microscope).
  • Later stages: Voids grow into fissures and cracks.
  • Advanced stages: Noticeable gross deformation or bulging (e.g., swollen heater tubes), though some low-ductility materials may crack without bulging.
Prevention
  • Creep is irreversible; damaged components must usually be repaired or replaced.
  • Minimize metal temperatures and avoid localized hot spots or flame impingement.
  • Select alloys with better creep resistance for high-temperature service.
  • Avoid stress concentrations during design and fabrication.
Inspection
  • Creep is difficult to detect before cracks form. Conventional NDT is largely ineffective for early stages.
  • Use dimensional measurements (strapping or gauging) to check for bulging in tubes.
  • Field Metallographic Replication (FMR) can detect surface voids/fissures.
  • Advanced UT techniques (PAUT, TOFD) or destructive sampling/metallography are used for advanced subsurface damage.
Related Short-term overheating (stress rupture), Stress Relaxation Cracking (SRC), and Dissimilar Metal Weld (DMW) cracking.

Description of Damage: A selective corrosion mechanism where one or more specific elements are preferentially leached out of an alloy, leaving behind a weak, porous, and lower-density structure.
Damage Type Dealloying
Affected Materials Primarily copper alloys (brasses, bronzes, copper-nickel alloys) and Alloy 400.
Main Cause Exposure to specific corrosive environments that selectively attack a constituent element (e.g., zinc in brass, nickel in Alloy 400).

  • Alloy composition and specific environmental conditions (temperature, pH).
  • Presence of oxygen or aeration heavily promotes the process.
  • Stagnant or low-flow conditions increase susceptibility.
Locations
  • Cooling water systems (tubes and tubesheets) using brackish or seawater.

  • Affected areas often exhibit a significant color change (e.g., yellow brass turning a porous, reddish-copper color).

  • Use "inhibited" copper alloys (e.g., admiralty brass with trace amounts of phosphorus, antimony, or tin) which resist dealloying.

  • Apply barrier coatings or cathodic protection.
  • Prevent oxygen entry into HF acid alkylation units.

  • Look for characteristic color changes (surface scale removal may be required).

Decarburization

Metallurgical Degradation
▼
Description of Damage: A condition where steel loses strength due to the removal of carbon and carbides, leaving behind a pure, weaker iron matrix.
Damage Type Decarburization
Affected Materials Carbon steels and low-alloy steels.
Main Cause Exposure to high temperatures, fires, or specific high-temperature gaseous environments that have a low carbon activity, causing carbon to diffuse out of the steel.
Critical Factors
  • Time, temperature, and the carbon activity of the process stream.
  • Shallow decarburization slightly decreases strength but is a strong indicator that the equipment was severely overheated.
Locations
  • Equipment exposed to fires or elevated service temperatures.
  • Pressure vessel components hot-formed during fabrication.
  • Piping, equipment, and fired heater tubes in hot hydrogen service (e.g., hydroprocessing or catalytic reforming units).

  • Cannot be seen with the naked eye; must be verified by metallography.

  • Follow API RP 941 guidelines when selecting steels for high-temperature hydrogen service.

  • Visual Testing (VT) is entirely ineffective.
  • Hardness testing acts as a good screening tool; the surface will be significantly softer than the base metal.
  • Field Metallographic Replication (FMR) can be used on accessible surfaces.
  • Accurate confirmation generally requires destructive sampling and laboratory metallographic examination.

Dissimilar Metal Weld (DMW) Cracking

Environmental Cracking
▼
Description of Damage: Cracking that occurs at the weld joints between two different metals. It is driven by differing thermal expansion rates, hard mixed-metal zones, environmental cracking, or hydrogen disbonding.
Damage Type Dissimilar Metal Weld Cracking
Affected Materials Commonly ferritic materials (carbon or low-alloy steels) welded to austenitic stainless steels (300 series) or nickel-based alloys. Also affects carbon steel welded to Alloy 400.
Main Cause Mismatched coefficients of thermal expansion causing shear stress, galvanic corrosion cells, or the creation of brittle "mixed zones" during the welding process.
Critical Factors
  • Thermal Expansion: High operating temperatures (usually >800°F/425°C) and thermal cycling cause high shear stress. Austenitic weld fillers cause more stress than nickel-based fillers. Carbon migration forms weak "soft zones".
  • Hard Zones/Environment: High-hardness mixed zones are susceptible to environmental cracking in wet H2S or HF acid service.
  • Weld geometry and stress concentrators exacerbate cracking.
Locations
  • Transitions in piping (e.g., clad pipe to solid stainless pipe).
  • Hydroprocessing exchanger inlet/outlet piping and fired heater internals.
  • Carbon steel to Alloy 400 welds in HF alkylation units.
  • 300 series SS weld overlays in reactors and pressure vessels.
Appearance
  • Thermal Expansion: Cracks typically form at the toe of the weld in the Heat-Affected Zone (HAZ) of the ferritic material.
  • Environmental: Cracks occur along the fusion line in the hard mixed-metal zone.
  • Hydrogen Disbonding: Cracks form directly at the interface between the weld metal and base metal.
Prevention
  • For high temperatures, use nickel-based filler metals (closer thermal expansion to carbon steel) or use an intermediate "pup piece".
  • Butter the ferritic side of the joint and apply PWHT before making the final attachment weld.
  • Avoid locating DMWs in severe environments (like wet H2S or HF acid); if unavoidable, locate them in low-stress/low-exposure areas.
Inspection
  • Requires expert evaluation. During fabrication, use Penetrant Testing (PT), Radiography (RT), or UT.
  • For creep damage, use advanced UT (PAUT, SWUT), Field Metallographic Replication (FMR), or destructive testing.
  • For environmental cracking, use PT or Wet Fluorescent Magnetic Particle Testing (WFMT) internally, or PAUT externally.
Related Thermal fatigue, corrosion fatigue, creep and stress rupture, wet H2S damage, and hydrogen stress cracking in HF.

Erosion/Erosion-Corrosion

Mechanical Degradation
▼
Description of Damage: Erosion is the physical abrasion of material by flowing solids, liquids, or vapors. Erosion-corrosion occurs when this high velocity flow removes protective scales or films, drastically accelerating the underlying corrosion rate.
Damage Type Erosion/Erosion-Corrosion
Affected Materials All metals and refractories. Primarily affects softer metals (like copper and aluminum alloys) and materials that rely on a protective corrosion layer/film for resistance.
Main Cause High-velocity flow of fluids, catalyst particles, slurries, or liquid droplets, often acting in tandem with a corrosive environment.
Critical Factors
  • Fluid velocity and the size, shape, hardness, and concentration of impacting particles.
  • Hardness of the metal component.
  • Fluid corrosivity (temperature, pH).
  • Some alloys have a specific "threshold velocity" where metal loss suddenly spikes if exceeded.
Locations
  • Piping bends (elbows, tees), downstream of letdown/block valves, and piping cross-section changes.
  • Pumps, impellers, agitators, cyclones, and slide valves.
  • Slurry piping in FCC units, coker heaters, and heat exchanger tubing.
Appearance
  • Localized thinning appearing as grooves, gullies, waves, valleys, or rounded holes.
  • Often exhibits a highly directional pattern aligned with the flow.
  • Common on the outer radius of piping elbows or the bottom of pipes where slurries tumble.
Prevention
  • Change geometry: increase pipe diameter to reduce flow velocity, streamline bends, or use impingement baffles.
  • For pure erosion: Increase component hardness, use hardfacing, or apply erosion-resistant refractories.
  • For erosion-corrosion: Upgrade to more corrosion-resistant alloys or alter the environment (deaeration, inhibitors). Increasing hardness alone will not stop erosion-corrosion.
Inspection
  • Perform UT thickness grids or automated scans specifically at susceptible areas (changes in direction/diameter). Random UT is ineffective.
  • Profile Radiography (RT) is useful for detecting eroded profiles.
  • Guided Wave Testing (GWT) can be used as a screening tool.
  • Infrared thermography can detect refractory degradation on-stream.

Environmental Cracking
▼
Description of Damage: Surface-initiated environmental cracking of carbon steel caused by the combined action of tensile stress and exposure to fuel-grade ethanol (FGE) or FGE/gasoline blends.
Damage Type Ethanol Stress Corrosion Cracking
Affected Materials All grades of carbon steel.
Main Cause The combined effects of high tensile stress and an aerated (oxygen-containing) ethanol environment.
Critical Factors
  • Aeration (Dissolved Oxygen): The most important factor; cracking does not occur in deaerated conditions.
  • Tensile stress (applied or residual) and stress concentrators (cold-worked components).
  • Water content (most severe between 0.1% and 4.5% volume).
  • Can occur in blends with as little as 20% FGE.
  • Galvanic coupling to previously corroded steel increases likelihood.
Locations
  • Carbon steel storage tanks (bottoms and floating roofs).
  • Loading rack piping and pipeline terminals handling FGE.
  • Note: Rarely found in end-use concentrations (10% FGE blends) or transportation equipment (barges, trucks).
Appearance
  • Cracks are typically tight, filled with corrosion products, and found in the vicinity of welds (running parallel or transverse to the weld).
  • Field failures are usually branched and intergranular.
  • High chloride levels can cause the cracks to shift to transgranular or mixed-mode cracking.
Prevention
  • Apply Post-Weld Heat Treatment (PWHT) or protective coatings.
  • Avoid designs with highly localized tensile stresses or lap seam welds.
  • Minimize cold working during fabrication (e.g., avoid cold-worked springs on floating roofs).
Inspection
  • Routine inspection is largely ineffective for management; efforts must focus on prevention.
  • When inspecting, focus on highly stressed areas, cold-worked zones, and weld HAZs.
  • Wet Fluorescent Magnetic Particle Testing (WFMT) is the preferred detection method.
  • Alternating Current Field Measurement (ACFM) and Angle Beam UT (SWUT, PAUT) can be used to detect and size cracks without internal access.

Flue Gas Dew Point Corrosion

Uniform / Localized Thinning
▼
Description of Damage: Severe corrosion that occurs when combustion gases (sulfur, nitrogen, chlorides) and water vapor condense into highly corrosive acids (sulfuric, sulfurous, hydrochloric, nitric) on cool metal surfaces.
Damage Type Flue Gas Dew Point Corrosion
Affected Materials Carbon steel, low-alloy steels, and 300 series stainless steels.
Main Cause Operating metal surface temperatures that drop at or below the dew point of the acid gases present in the flue gas.
Critical Factors
  • Concentration of contaminants (sulfur, nitrogen, chlorides) in the fuel.
  • Sulfuric acid has the highest dew point (typically ~280°F / 140°C) and is the most common cause.
  • Other acids (hydrochloric, nitric, sulfurous) condense at lower temperatures, closer to the water dew point (212°F / 100°C).
Locations
  • Economizer sections and exhaust stacks of fired process heaters and boilers.
  • Sulfur Recovery Unit (SRU) incinerators and FCC regenerator flue gas systems.
  • Heat-Recovery Steam Generators (HRSGs) and oil-fired boilers during water washing.
Appearance
  • Carbon/low-alloy steels: General overall wastage and thinning, often accompanied by broad, shallow pits.
  • 300 series SS (in HRSGs): Chloride Stress Corrosion Cracking (SCC) showing surface-breaking cracks with a crazed appearance.
Prevention
  • Keep metallic surfaces at the back end of boilers and stacks at least 10°F (6°C) above the sulfuric acid dew point.
  • Avoid using 300 series SS in HRSG feedwater heaters if chlorides/chlorine are present in the environment (e.g., from cooling tower drift).
  • When water-washing ash from boilers, use an alkaline sodium carbonate final rinse to neutralize acid salts.
Inspection
  • Use UT thickness measurements or permanently mounted sensors to monitor wastage.
  • On finned economizer tubes, sections of the fins usually must be removed to take UT readings.
  • Use Radiography (RT) for thickness readings where geometry allows.
  • For 300 series SS, use VT, PT, and Eddy Current Testing (ECT) to find SCC.
Related Hydrochloric acid corrosion, sulfuric acid corrosion, and Chloride SCC.

Fuel Ash Corrosion

Uniform / Localized Thinning
▼
Description of Damage: Accelerated high-temperature wastage that occurs when fuel contaminants (sulfur, sodium, potassium, vanadium) form deposits that melt into highly corrosive liquid slags on metal surfaces.
Damage Type Fuel Ash Corrosion
Affected Materials All conventional alloys used for process heaters and boiler construction. Alloys of the 50Cr-50Ni family show improved resistance.
Main Cause Firing fuels (like heavy oil or coal) that contain sodium and vanadium (or potassium), which form molten salts that dissolve the metal's protective oxide scale.
Critical Factors
  • Concentration of contaminants (a combined Na + V content > 50 ppm causes damage).
  • Metal temperature: Corrosion only occurs if the metal is hot enough to melt the deposits into a liquid slag.
  • Reducing atmospheres (low-oxygen) in coal boilers dramatically increase corrosion rates.
Locations
  • Fired heaters, boilers, and gas turbines burning contaminated fuel oils, residues, or coal.
  • Tube hangers and supports (which operate hotter than tubes) are highly susceptible.
  • Superheaters, reheaters, and waterwall tubes.
Appearance
  • Oil ash: Severe metal loss with numerous deep, round pits and a hard, glassy, tenacious scale/slag.
  • Coal ash: A smooth interface under a glassy slag layer, leaving an "alligator-hide" appearance when the scale is removed.
  • Waterwalls: Circumferential grooving caused by combined thermal fatigue cracking and corrosion.
Prevention
  • Blend or change fuel sources to minimize sodium and vanadium contaminants.
  • Operate equipment below the temperature where molten deposits form.
  • Improve burner design to prevent localized hot spots and flame impingement.
  • Use 50Cr-50Ni alloys for tube hangers and supports.
  • Inject fuel additives to raise the melting point of the slags.
Inspection
  • Visual Testing (VT) is usually sufficient to identify severe metal loss and the presence of slag.
  • Grit blasting may be required to remove the tenacious, glass-like slag deposits before inspection.
  • Use UT to measure the remaining wall thickness.
Related Hot corrosion, hot ash corrosion, molten salt corrosion, oil ash corrosion, and coal ash corrosion.

Galvanic Corrosion

Uniform / Localized Thinning
▼
Description of Damage: Accelerated corrosion that occurs when two dissimilar metals are joined together in a conductive electrolyte (like moist soil or water).
Damage Type Galvanic Corrosion
Affected Materials All metals except most noble metals.
Main Cause Difference in electrochemical potential between two connected metals in an electrolyte, causing the more active metal (anode) to corrode faster.
Critical Factors
  • Requires three conditions: two different metals, electrical connection, and a continuous electrolyte.
  • Anode-to-cathode surface area ratio (small anode + large cathode = very high corrosion).
  • Position of alloys on the galvanic series (farther apart = higher driving force).
Locations Any unit where different metals are coupled in conductive fluids. Common in heat exchangers (tubes vs. tubesheets/baffles), buried piping, and ship hulls.
Appearance Damage happens where materials are joined. The more active metal suffers generalized thinning or localized crevice/grooving/pitting adjacent to the connection.
Prevention
  • Good design: avoid coupling different metals in conductive fluids.
  • Ensure favorable anode-to-cathode surface area ratio.
  • If coating, coat BOTH metals or just the noble (cathode) metal. Do NOT coat only the active metal.
  • Use insulating bolt sleeves/gaskets to break electrical connections.
  • Use sacrificial anodes (e.g., zinc or magnesium).
Inspection Visual testing (VT) and ultrasonic thickness (UT) gauging for detecting thickness loss. Angle beam UT may be needed for hidden damage under bolts/rivets.
Related Concentration cell corrosion, soil corrosion, DMW cracking, titanium hydriding.

Metallurgical Degradation
▼
Description of Damage: Damage caused when hydrogen diffuses into steel at high temperatures and pressures, reacting with carbon/carbides to form methane (CH4) gas, which cannot escape and builds internal pressure.
Damage Type Internal Cracking / Metallurgical Degradation
Affected Materials Carbon steel, C-0.5Mo, and lower Cr-Mo steels. (300 series SS and higher Cr alloys like 5Cr/9Cr are not susceptible.)

  • Temperature, hydrogen partial pressure, and time.
  • Alloy content (Chromium and Molybdenum stabilize carbides, increasing resistance).

Prevention
  • Use alloy steels with Chromium and Molybdenum (which stabilize carbides).
  • Operate within the safe limits of the API RP 941 curves (with a safety factor).
  • Avoid C-0.5Mo steel for new construction in hot hydrogen service.
  • Apply Post Weld Heat Treatment (PWHT) to reduce stress.
Inspection Very difficult to detect in early stages. Requires highly specialized NDE (like TOFD and PAUT). Standard UT/RT are not reliable for HTHA.

Hydrochloric Acid Corrosion

Uniform / Localized Thinning
▼
Description of Damage: Very aggressive corrosion caused by aqueous hydrochloric acid (HCl), often occurring when water and hydrogen chloride gas condense together.
Damage Type General and Localized Corrosion
Affected Materials All common materials used in refineries. Carbon steel, 300 series SS, and 400 series SS are all highly susceptible. (Alloy 400 and Titanium have better resistance.)
Main Cause Condensation of HCl gas with water (acid dew point) or formation of aqueous HCl beneath chloride salt deposits.
Critical Factors
  • Acid concentration and temperature (higher = worse).
  • pH dropping below 4.5 causes excessive corrosion in carbon steel.
  • Presence of oxidizing agents (oxygen, ferric ions) severely increases corrosion for Alloy 400.

  • Optimize crude desalting to reduce chloride feed (< 20 ppm).
  • Use water washing to dilute condensing acid.
  • Inject neutralizing and filming amines.
  • Upgrade to Titanium or Nickel-based alloys (Alloy 400).
Inspection Visual inspection for orange-yellow discoloration and scale. UT thickness mapping and Profile RT (especially at elbows, dead-legs, and mixing points). Regularly monitor water pH and chloride content.

Hydrogen Embrittlement

Mechanical Degradation
▼
Description of Damage: The loss of strength, ductility, and fracture toughness in susceptible metals due to the penetration and diffusion of atomic hydrogen, leading to brittle cracking.
Damage Type Loss of Toughness / Brittle Cracking
Affected Materials Low-alloy steels, high-strength steels, 400 series SS, and some Nickel alloys. Carbon steel is susceptible if hardened by cold work/welding (usually hardness > HRC 22).
Main Cause Atomic hydrogen entering the metal from welding (wet electrodes), corrosion reactions (wet H2S, HF), high-temp hydrogen gas, or manufacturing (electroplating).
Critical Factors
  • Requires 5 conditions: susceptible material, critical hydrogen concentration, high strength/hardness, sufficient tensile stress, and embrittling temperature.
  • Most severe at ambient temperatures; effect disappears above ~300 °F (150 °C).
  • Reversible if cracking hasn't occurred (hydrogen can be baked out).
Locations Hydroprocessing/catalytic reforming reactors, high-strength storage spheres, high-strength bolts/springs, and equipment in wet H2S or HF service.
Appearance Not visible until brittle cracking occurs. Cracks are often surface-breaking, branched, and occur at stress concentrations or hard HAZs.
Prevention
  • Control hardness levels (e.g., via PWHT) and use lower-strength alloys.
  • Use dry, low-hydrogen welding electrodes.
  • Perform elevated temperature bake-outs (e.g., 600 °F) to drive out hydrogen.
  • Apply protective linings/claddings to prevent hydrogen entry from corrosion.
Inspection PT, MT, or WFMT for surface cracking. Angle beam UT for sizing cracks. RT and VT are not reliable.
Related Underbead cracking, Sulfide Stress Cracking (SSC), Hydrogen Stress Cracking in HF.

Hydrogen Stress Cracking in Hydrofluoric Acid

Environmental Cracking
▼
Description of Damage: Environmental cracking in high-strength low-alloy and carbon steels caused by exposure to aqueous HF acid, typically occurring in highly localized, high-hardness zones.
Damage Type Hydrogen Stress Cracking
Affected Materials Carbon steel and low-alloy steels.
Main Cause Embrittlement from hydrogen charged into the steel by the corrosion reaction in HF, exacerbated by high applied or residual tensile stress.
Critical Factors
  • Steel hardness, strength, and stress levels.
  • Susceptibility increases with hardness; levels above HRC 22 (HB 237) are highly vulnerable.
  • Higher-strength steels are more susceptible and fail faster.
  • High residual tensile stress from cold-forming or welding promotes cracking.
  • Cracking can happen rapidly (hours) or take considerable time.
Locations
  • Piping and equipment exposed to HF acid at any concentration with hardness levels above limits.
  • High-strength low-alloy steel components (e.g., ASTM A193-B7 bolts, valve parts, compressor parts).
  • Overtorqued ASTM A193-B7M bolts.
Appearance
  • Surface-breaking cracks, usually associated with weldments.
  • Primarily transgranular in carbon steel; intergranular in high-strength steel.
  • Can only be confirmed by metallographic examination.
Prevention
  • Use weld procedure qualification, chemistry controls, and Post Weld Heat Treatment (PWHT) to avoid high-hardness HAZs.
  • Use softer ASTM A193 grade B7M bolts instead of B7 bolts.
  • Apply alloy cladding or nonmetallic coatings to protect steel surfaces.
  • Avoid using Alloy 400 without stress relief to prevent SCC.
Inspection
  • Detect surface cracks using PT, MT, or WFMT.
  • Use Angle Beam UT (SWUT or PAUT) for crack depth, and Straight Beam UT for cracked bolts.
  • Perform hardness testing on weld metal and bolting to check susceptibility.

3.42

Liquid Metal Embrittlement

Environmental Cracking
▼
Description of Damage: Sudden and brittle cracking occurring when specific alloys come in contact with certain molten or liquid metals.
Damage Type Liquid Metal Embrittlement (LME)
Affected Materials Many alloys including carbon steel, low-alloy steels, high-strength steels, 300 series SS, nickel alloys, copper alloys, aluminum alloys, and titanium alloys.
Main Cause Contact between specific metal combinations and low-melting-point metals (e.g., zinc, mercury, cadmium, lead).
Critical Factors
  • Requires specific metal/embrittler combinations.
  • Initiates upon contact; very small quantities of liquid metal are enough.
  • High tensile stress speeds up crack propagation rapidly, sometimes within seconds.
  • Fracture can occur with almost zero percent strain (very brittle).
  • Can happen later if the susceptible metal is contaminated at low temperatures but later heated above the embrittler's melting point.
Locations
  • Most common after a fire, when molten metals (like zinc galvanizing, cadmium, lead, or copper) drip onto susceptible metals.
  • 300 series SS piping near galvanized steel during a fire.
  • Equipment exposed to mercury leaks from instruments or mercury-containing crude oil.
  • Aluminum components in LNG or cryogenic gas plants exposed to liquid mercury.
Appearance
  • Brittle cracks in an otherwise ductile material.
  • Confirmed via metallography showing intergranular cracks often filled with the low-melting metal.
Prevention
  • Protect metal surfaces from coming into contact with low-melting metals.
  • Avoid welding galvanized steel components to 300 series SS.
  • Protect 300 series SS from overspray of zinc-containing coatings.
  • Note: Grinding out the affected area is usually not a practical fix once cracked.
Inspection
  • Visual inspection for obvious cracks and solidified molten metal deposits.
  • Use MT or WFMT for ferritic materials (e.g., carbon steel).
  • Use PT for nonmagnetic materials.
  • Use Radiographic Testing (RT) or a boroscope to locate high-density mercury deposits inside equipment.

Mechanical Fatigue (Including Vibration-induced Fatigue)

Mechanical Degradation
▼
Description of Damage: A mechanical form of degradation resulting in sudden, unexpected cracking caused by extended exposure to cyclical stresses (e.g., dynamic loading, vibration, water hammer).
Damage Type Mechanical Fatigue
Affected Materials All engineering alloys.
Main Cause Cyclical stresses from mechanical loading, often amplified by stress risers or resonant frequencies.
Critical Factors
  • Component geometry, stress level, number of cycles, and material properties.
  • High likelihood of cracking when vibrational load synchronizes with the component's natural frequency.
  • Initiates at surface notches, sharp corners, weld joint flaws, tool marks, or corrosion spots.
  • Lack of proper support or stiffening in vibrating equipment leads to cracking.
  • Some materials (e.g., carbon steel) have an "endurance limit" below which fatigue won't occur, while others (like 300 series SS) do not.
Locations
  • Socket welds and small-bore piping near pumps or compressors lacking proper support.
  • Unsupported valves or controllers on small branch connections.
  • Safety-relief valves subject to chatter.
  • Piping connected to high-pressure-drop control valves.
  • Rotating shafts with stress concentrations.
  • Heat exchanger tubes susceptible to vortex shedding.
Appearance
  • Cracks emanating from high stress concentration points.
  • Signature "clam-shell" fingerprint or "beach marks" visible on the fracture surface.
  • Single nucleation site for a specific stress defect, or multiple sites ("ratchet markings") for generalized overstress.
Prevention
  • Improve design to minimize stress concentrations in cyclic service.
  • Provide proper support, gussets, or vibration dampeners for piping and small connections.
  • Minimize vortex shedding at valve outlets using proper sizing.
  • Ensure smooth transitions and avoid weld defects or grinding marks.
  • Use generous radii in rotating equipment keyways.
Inspection
  • Routine Non-Destructive Examination (NDE) is usually impractical because cracks propagate rapidly once detectable.
  • Use PT, MT, or WFMT for surface cracks.
  • Use Angle Beam UT (SWUT or PAUT) for internal or suspected problem areas.
  • Perform vibration monitoring on rotating equipment.
  • Visually inspect pipe supports, spring hangers, and listen for audible vibration sounds.
Related Thermal Fatigue, Corrosion Fatigue.

Metal Dusting

Metallurgical Degradation
▼
Description of Damage: Accelerated localized pitting and rapid metal wastage in processes containing carbon and oxygen, resulting in pits filled with carbon soot and metal dust.
Damage Type Metal Dusting
Affected Materials Low-alloy steels, 300 series SS, nickel-based alloys, and heat-resisting alloys. No known alloy is completely immune.
Main Cause Reducing environments with high carbon activity (>1), leading to carburization and decomposition of metal carbides into dust.
Critical Factors
  • Process stream composition, operating temperature, and alloy composition.
  • Typically occurs between 900 °F and 1500 °F (480 °C to 815 °C).
  • Mechanism involves carburization, carbide precipitation, graphite deposition, and carbide decomposition.
  • Preferentially attacks grain boundaries.
Locations
  • Syn gas generation processes and steam reforming heaters.
  • Gas turbines, methanol reforming unit outlet piping, thermal hydrodealkylation furnaces.
  • Outlets of steam reformer heaters and POX units.
Appearance
  • Severe localized pitting filled with a crumbly residue of metal particles and carbon soot.
  • Often swept away by flow, leaving thinned or pitted metal.
  • Appears as "green rot" in cast alloys (interdendritic penetration).
  • Metallography shows carburization under the attacked surface.
Prevention
  • Maintain a low level of reactive sulfur (like H2S) in the environment to inhibit carbon absorption.
  • Use refractory lining to keep metal temperatures below the dusting range.
  • Apply aluminum diffusion treatments (e.g., alonizing).
  • Control process composition (e.g., maintain a high steam-to-carbon ratio) to keep carbon activity below 1.
Inspection
  • Often noticed only after failure due to rapid rates.
  • Destructive testing and metallography are most accurate.
  • Visual Testing (VT) of accessible internal surfaces for severe pitting or thinning.
  • Radiographic Testing (RT) for pitting and wall thinning.
  • Check filters for metal particles indicating upstream dusting.

Microbiologically Influenced Corrosion

Uniform / Localized Thinning
▼
Description of Damage: Localized corrosion (pitting or crevice) caused by living organisms like bacteria, algae, or fungi, often associated with tubercles or slime.
Damage Type Microbiologically Influenced Corrosion (MIC)
Affected Materials Carbon and alloy steels, 300 and 400 series SS, aluminum alloys, copper alloys, and some nickel alloys. Titanium is highly resistant.
Main Cause Colonies of microorganisms thriving in aqueous environments, producing corrosive by-products.
Critical Factors
  • Requires aqueous environments, often stagnant or low-flow conditions.
  • Organisms survive extreme conditions (lack of oxygen, varied pH and temperature).
  • Driven by nutrients like sulfur, ammonia, H2S, hydrocarbons, and organic acids.
  • Contaminants like hydrocarbons can trigger massive biofouling.
Locations
  • Water-cooled heat exchangers and bottom water layers of storage tanks.
  • Stagnant or low-flow piping.
  • Systems containing left-over hydrotest water.
  • Fire-water systems and drain systems near docks.
Appearance
  • Localized pitting under deposits or tubercles.
  • Characterized by cup-shaped pits within pits ("halo effect") in carbon steel, or subsurface cavities in stainless steel.
  • Often indistinguishable from regular under-deposit or chloride pitting without further testing.
Prevention
  • Treat water systems with biocides (chlorine, bromine, ozone, UV) continuously.
  • Periodically flush and clean susceptible systems to remove deposits.
  • Maintain flow rates above 3 fps (1 m/s) to inhibit colony formation.
  • Keep non-water systems clean, dry, and sloped for drainage.
  • Empty hydrotest water promptly and dry the equipment.
  • Use coatings and cathodic protection for underground or tank interior surfaces.
Inspection
  • Monitor cooling water for biocide residual, microbe counts (ATP, qPCR), and visual appearance.
  • Use special fouling probes or corrosion coupons.
  • Monitor for loss of heat exchanger duty or foul-smelling water.
  • Use localized NDE methods like VT, AUT, or RT to find pitting.

Oxygenated Process Water Corrosion

Uniform / Localized Thinning
▼
Description of Damage: Significantly increased corrosion activity and pitting caused by the presence of dissolved oxygen in process water streams.
Damage Type Oxygenated Process Water Corrosion
Affected Materials Carbon steel and low-alloy steels.
Main Cause Oxygen dissolving into liquid water, interfering with protective iron oxide formation and accelerating corrosion.
Critical Factors
  • Requires liquid water and oxygen.
  • Only trace levels of oxygen (~20 ppb) are needed to spike corrosion rates.
  • Worse at lower temperatures due to higher oxygen solubility in cool water.
  • High velocity and turbulence (>10 fps) interfere with protective scale, causing rapid localized attack.
Locations
  • Bottom of piping where water phases separate, or top of piping where condensation occurs.
  • Turbulent areas: downstream of welds, elbows, valves, or ejectors.
  • Vacuum units where air leaks into the system (especially steam ejector systems).
  • Low points in compressor inlet piping and flare gas recovery units.
Appearance
  • Extensive general pitting on wetted surfaces.
  • Localized pitting in low-velocity areas.
  • Accelerated localized corrosion at the back of elbows or turbulent zones where protective scales fail to form.
Prevention
  • Strictly limit oxygen/air ingress into non-aerated systems (fix vacuum leaks).
  • Carbon steel is acceptable if oxygen is kept below 20 ppb.
  • Use internal vessel coatings designed for the specific service and temperature.
  • Consider oxygen scavengers or filming amines only as a last resort if leaks cannot be fixed.
Inspection
  • Focus on high-velocity/turbulent areas as well as stagnant zones.
  • Check bottoms of pipes for water pooling and tops for condensation.
  • Inspect full pipe circumferences using RT, manual/grid UT, or AUT.
  • Use permanent thickness sensors.
  • Consult experts for sampling water to measure ultra-low O2 levels.

Damage Type Phenol (Carbolic Acid) Corrosion
Affected Materials Carbon steel, 304L SS. More resistant are 316L SS and Alloy C276.
Main Cause Exposure to hot phenol solutions, exacerbated by water content and high flow velocities.
Critical Factors
  • Temperature, water content, velocity, and alloy chemistry.
  • Carbon steel corrosion exceeds 20 mpy above 350 °F (175 °C) and is very rapid above 500 °F (260 °C).
  • Dilute aqueous solutions (5% to 15% phenol) are highly corrosive.
  • High velocities (>30 ft/s) cause severe erosion-corrosion.
Locations
  • Phenol extraction facilities in lube oil plants.
  • Recovery sections where spent phenol is vaporized.
  • Extract dryer condensers exposed to dilute aqueous solutions.
  • Overhead circuits suffering from erosion-corrosion or condensation corrosion.
Appearance
  • General thinning or localized corrosion, often appearing as erosion-corrosion.
Prevention
  • Proper materials selection and solvent chemistry control.
  • Limit velocities in carbon steel to <30 ft/s.
  • Maintain recovery tower overhead temperatures at least 30 °F (15 °C) above the dew point.
  • Upgrade to 316L SS for dryer towers, flash towers, condensers, and extract furnace tubes.
  • Use Alloy C276 in extremely high-velocity areas.
Inspection
  • VT of accessible internal surfaces at liquid impingement or turbulent areas.
  • Follow up with UT thickness measurements on suspect external areas.
  • RT to identify localized attack on smaller pipes.
  • Use permanently mounted thickness sensors, ER probes, or corrosion coupons.

▼
Description of Damage: Rapid intergranular stress corrosion cracking of sensitized austenitic stainless steels and some nickel alloys. It happens when sulfide scale is exposed to air (oxygen) and moisture during shutdowns or start-ups, forming polythionic acids.
Damage Type Polythionic Acid Stress Corrosion Cracking (PTA SCC)

  • Requires a combination of susceptible (sensitized) material, high tensile stress, and the specific environment.
  • Sensitization occurs at 700 °F–1500 °F (370 °C–815 °C).
  • Residual stresses from non-stress-relieved welds are the most common source of stress.
  • High-carbon grades (304H/316H) are very susceptible.
Locations
  • Units using sensitized alloys in high-temperature sulfur environments (e.g., Hydroprocessing, FCC, Crude, Coker).
  • Usually adjacent to welds or high-stress areas (heater tubes, piping, bellows).

Prevention
  • Flush equipment with alkaline/soda ash solution during shutdown to neutralize acids.
  • Purge with dry nitrogen to prevent air exposure.
  • Keep fireboxes heated above dew point.
  • Use low-carbon (L) or chemically stabilized grades (Types 321, 347).
  • Thermal stabilization heat treatment.
Inspection
  • PT examination is most common (may require grinding to remove tight deposits).
  • ECT for surface cracks.
  • Angle beam UT (SWUT/PAUT) depending on thickness.
  • Prevention is key, as cracking only occurs during shutdowns.

▼
Description of Damage: Intergranular cracking in the creep temperature range during post-weld heat treatment (PWHT) or high-temperature service. It happens when creep ductility cannot accommodate strains needed to relieve applied/residual stresses.

  • Exposure to creep temperatures.
  • High residual stresses from welding or cold working.

  • Hot-wall 1¼Cr-½Mo and 1¼Cr-½Mo vessels and piping operating above 900 °F (480 °C) (e.g., Catalytic reformers).

  • Design joints to minimize stress concentrations and restraint.
  • Avoid sharp changes in cross-section.
  • Apply adequate preheat and follow recommended PWHT guidelines.
  • Use low heat input welding to avoid grain coarsening.
  • Use specific weld material screening for vulnerable alloys like 2¼Cr-1Mo-V.
Inspection
  • WFMT, MT, or PT for surface cracks (depending on the material).
  • Angle beam UT (SWUT, PAUT) or TOFD for embedded cracks.

Sigma Phase Embrittlement

Metallurgical Degradation
▼
Description of Damage: A metallurgical change forming a hard, brittle intermetallic compound (sigma phase) in certain stainless steels when exposed to high temperatures. It causes a loss of ductility and fracture toughness at lower temperatures.
Damage Type Sigma Phase Embrittlement
Affected Materials 300 series SS (especially cast alloys like HK/HP with high ferrite), 400 series SS, and duplex stainless steels.
Main Cause Prolonged exposure to temperatures in the range of 1000 °F to 1700 °F (540 °C to 925 °C), leading to precipitation of the sigma phase.
Critical Factors
  • Time at elevated temperature is the primary factor.
  • Alloy composition: High chromium/molybdenum and high ferrite content accelerate sigma formation.
  • Cooling below 500 °F (260 °C) triggers extreme brittleness, while the material retains some ductility at operating temperatures.
Locations
  • High-temperature FCC regenerator internals (cyclones, piping, valves).
  • Stainless steel heater tubes.
  • 300 series SS weld overlays and tube-to-tubesheet welds subjected to PWHT.

Prevention
  • Use resistant alloys or avoid embrittling temperature ranges.
  • Avoid applying high stresses to embrittled equipment during shutdowns.
  • Control ferrite content in welds (e.g., 5-9% for Type 347) to minimize sigma while preventing hot cracking.
  • Minimize exposure of weld overlays to high PWHT temperatures.
Inspection
  • Lab testing (metallography/impact testing) of removed samples.
  • Field metallographic replication (FMR) for surface presence.
  • Eddy Current Testing (ECT) for duplex stainless steels (dependent on operator skill).

▼
Description of Damage: Corrosion of steel caused by acidic sour water containing hydrogen sulfide (H2S) with a pH between 4.5 and 7.0.
Damage Type Sour Water Corrosion (Acidic)
Affected Materials Primarily carbon steel. Stainless steels, copper alloys, and nickel-based alloys are usually resistant.

  • Use water wash injection to dilute corrodents, sometimes with polysulfide to neutralize cyanides.

  • UT scanning and RT for localized thinning.
  • Permanent thickness monitoring sensors.
  • Continuous corrosion probes/coupons for early warning (though iron sulfide scale can mislead probes).

Strain Aging

Metallurgical Degradation
▼
Description of Damage: A form of metallurgical damage in older, non-fully deoxidized carbon steels that undergo plastic deformation followed by aging at intermediate temperatures. It causes increased hardness/strength but a severe loss of ductility and toughness, leading to brittle fracture risks.
Damage Type Strain Aging
Affected Materials Older (pre-1980) carbon steels with large grain size and C-0.5Mo low-alloy steels (made via Bessemer or open hearth processes).
Main Cause Interstitial nitrogen and carbon impurities pinning dislocations after cold working and exposure to intermediate temperatures.
Critical Factors
  • Steel manufacturing process (high nitrogen levels from air-blown processes).
  • Modern basic oxygen furnace, fully aluminum-killed steels are not susceptible.
  • Occurs when materials are deformed (cold worked or cracked) and heated without stress relief.
  • Dynamic strain aging occurs during the heat and stress of the welding cycle itself.
Locations
  • Thick wall vessels made of susceptible older materials that were not stress relieved.
  • Near stress concentrations, closely spaced welds, or repair welds.
Appearance Not visually apparent until a brittle fracture occurs. Can be identified by an increase in hardness in the deformed/aged zone or via detailed metallurgical analysis.
Prevention
  • Use modern, fully deoxidized (killed) steels with low impurities.
  • For older equipment, control pressurization sequences to ensure the metal temperature is high enough to avoid brittle fracture.
  • Apply PWHT to weld repairs (1100 °F–1200 °F) to eliminate embrittling strains. Use buttering if PWHT is impossible.
Inspection
  • No online NDE techniques exist to directly detect strain aging.
  • Hardness testing can detect it over large areas but is ineffective for localized weld-related strain aging.

▼

Affected Materials All materials of construction.

  • Control heating/cooling rates.
  • Install thermal sleeves or liners to prevent cold liquid contacting hot boundary wall.

  • Surface: PT, MT, WFMT, ECT, ACFM, EMAT.

3.65

Thermal Shock

Mechanical Degradation
▼
Description of Damage: Cracking occurring when high, non-uniform thermal stresses develop in a single event over a short time due to restrained differential expansion/contraction (usually cold liquid on hot metal).
Damage Type Thermal Shock
Affected Materials All metals and alloys.
Main Cause Large and rapid temperature changes (quenching) on a constrained component.
Critical Factors
  • Magnitude of temperature differential and coefficient of thermal expansion (stainless steels have higher coefficients, so higher stresses).
  • High-temp exposure (fire) followed by water quench.
  • Constraint preventing expansion/contraction.
  • More prevalent in thick sections (castings). Loss of ductility (temper embrittlement) makes it worse.
Locations High-temp units: FCC, coker, catalytic reforming, high-severity hydroprocessing. Heavy wall machinery (thick castings). Any hot equipment exposed to rain/fire-water deluge.
Appearance Surface initiating cracks, may appear as "craze" cracks. Highly localized.
Prevention
  • Minimize situations where cold water (rain/fire-water) contacts hot equipment.
  • Design to minimize severe restraint.
  • Install thermal sleeves/protective measures.
Inspection Rare and unpredictable (usually after a specific event like a fire). Difficult to locate. VT (hard to differentiate from oxide craze), PT, MT, UT.
Related Thermal fatigue (3.64) and short-term overheating—stress rupture (3.55).
3.66

Titanium Hydriding

Refining Specific Degradation
▼
Description of Damage: A metallurgical phenomenon where hydrogen diffuses into titanium and forms an embrittling hydride phase, causing complete loss of ductility and toughness.
Damage Type Titanium Hydriding
Affected Materials Titanium and titanium alloys.
Main Cause Absorption of hydrogen into the titanium matrix.
Critical Factors
  • Metal temperature > 165 °F (75 °C).
  • Solution chemistry: pH < 3, pH > 8, or neutral pH with high H2S.
  • Galvanic contact with more active metals (like carbon steel).
  • Iron sulfide scale from upstream equipment.
  • Hydrogen solubility limits (alpha-beta alloys limited to 50-300 ppm).
Locations Heat exchangers and air coolers (SWS, amine units). Overhead condensers. Piping > 165 °F. Cathodic protection potentials < -0.9 v SCE.
Appearance Metallurgical change not visually apparent (requires microscopy). Cracking/leaks will be obvious. Tubes may remain intact until disturbed/removed, then crack or shatter like glass. Can ignite/combust!
Prevention
  • Avoid galvanic couples (use all titanium or electrically isolate).
  • Take precautions during outages to avoid breaking embrittled parts.
  • Consider risks in known hydriding services (amine, SWS).
Inspection
  • VT for cracks.
  • Sampling and destructive testing (microstructural analysis) needed to confirm hydriding.
  • Bend/crush/hammer test (embrittled part shatters like glass).
Related Hydrogen Embrittlement (3.40), Brittle Fracture (3.11).

3.67

Wet H2S Damage (Blistering/HIC/SOHIC/SSC)

Environmental Cracking
▼
Description of Damage: Four related types of damage in wet H2S environments caused by hydrogen atoms entering steel. Blistering (bulges), Hydrogen-Induced Cracking (HIC - stepwise internal cracking), Stress-Oriented HIC (SOHIC - stacked cracks forming thru-wall), and Sulfide Stress Cracking (SSC - cracking under stress/corrosion).
Damage Type Wet H2S Damage (Blistering, HIC, SOHIC, and SSC)
Affected Materials Carbon steel. For SSC: low-alloy steels and martensitic stainless steels.
Main Cause Hydrogen atoms from corrosion reactions diffuse into steel (H2S poisons recombination, letting atomic hydrogen enter). They recombine inside to form H2 gas pressure (Blistering/HIC) or interact with stress (SOHIC/SSC).
Critical Factors
  • Liquid water with H2S must be present.
  • H2S levels and low pH (<4) or high alkaline pH (with cyanide/ammonia) increase charging.
  • Contaminants like HCN significantly increase corrosion/charging.
  • Microstructure: inclusions/laminations worsen Blistering/HIC.
  • Hardness > 200 HB and high stress drive SSC.
Locations Anywhere with wet H2S. FCC/coker vapor recovery (HCN is present). SWS and amine regenerators. Hydroprocessing (if ammonium bisulfide > 2%). Hard welds and high-strength components.
Appearance
  • Blisters: visible bulges on ID.
  • HIC: internal stepwise cracks.
  • SOHIC: stacked cracks near weld HAZs.
  • SSC: cracking in hard welds/high-strength components.
Prevention
  • Barrier coatings/cladding.
  • Wash water/polysulfide injection to dilute/convert cyanides.
  • HIC-resistant steels.
  • PWHT (helps SOHIC and relieves stress for SSC).
  • Limit weld hardness (<200 HB) for SSC.
Inspection
  • VT for blisters.
  • WFMT, ECT, ACFM for SOHIC/surface cracks.
  • SWUT/PAUT for volumetric/internal cracks.
  • Monitor water phase for pH/cyanide/ammonia.
Related Hydrogen Embrittlement (3.40), Hydrogen Stress Cracking in HF (3.41), DMW cracking (3.26), Amine cracking (3.3), Carbonate cracking (3.12).
