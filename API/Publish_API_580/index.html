Section 1

Scope

API RP 580
▼
Executive Summary: API RP 580 provides the foundational framework and essential minimum requirements for developing, implementing, and sustaining a valid Risk-Based Inspection (RBI) program for fixed equipment in refining, petrochemical, and chemical process industries.
Clause Requirement Detailed Guidance & Engineering Methodology Key Considerations & Scope Boundaries
1.1 Industry Scope Should Specifies the application of RBI primarily to the hydrocarbon and chemical process industries, including oil and gas production, refining, petrochemical, and chemical manufacturing. Can be adapted for other continuous process industries handling hazardous materials. Focuses on plants containing pressurized systems where loss of containment presents safety, health, environmental, or business interruption consequences.
1.2 Purpose Shall Provides owner-operators with the core elements for developing an RBI program to:
  • Systematically identify active and potential damage mechanisms (DMR).
  • Assess the Probability of Failure (POF) and Consequence of Failure (COF).
  • Prioritize equipment based on calculated or categorized risk.
  • Formulate customized, risk-targeted inspection plans (what, where, how, when).
 Mandatory Principle: An RBI program must be a continuous improvement cycle (plan-do-check-act), not a one-time static calculation.
1.3 Flexibility in Application May Permits flexibility across the spectrum of assessment methodologies:
  • Qualitative: Descriptive grading (High/Med/Low) based on engineering judgment.
  • Semi-Quantitative: Blended scoring combining structured logic trees with numerical ranges.
  • Quantitative: Rigorous numerical modeling with probabilistic event trees and dispersion physics.
 Users may apply different methods to different units or equipment within the same facility depending on hazard level and available data.
1.4 Mechanical Integrity Focused Shall Strictly covers pressure boundary integrity for fixed equipment:
  • Pressure vessels (reactors, columns, drums, heat exchangers).
  • Piping systems and associated components.
  • Atmospheric and low-pressure storage tanks.
  • Pressure relief devices (PRDs).
 Exclusions: Does NOT replace structural steel, electrical, or instrument safety systems (SIS) integrity programs, though RBI outputs can interface with them.
1.5 Target Audience Should Written for owner-operator personnel responsible for mechanical integrity: inspection engineers, corrosion specialists, plant engineers, operations/maintenance leadership, and risk practitioners. Promotes cross-functional collaboration rather than isolated inspection department execution.
1.6 Benefits & Limitations Key for Success Benefits:
  • Systematic reduction of catastrophic loss of containment risk.
  • Optimization of turnaround (TAR) work scope and internal inspections.
  • Targeted non-destructive examination (NDE) methods matching damage mechanisms.
  • Justified extension of inspection intervals where risk is demonstrably low.
Limitations:
  • Cannot eliminate risk entirely (residual risk always remains).
  • Subject to "garbage-in, garbage-out" (data errors invalidate results).
  • Does not prevent unexpected mechanism attacks if operating limits (IOWs) are breached.
 Requires active management commitment and rigorous adherence to Management of Change (MOC).

Section 4

Risk Assessment Concepts

API RP 580
▼
Fundamental Risk Concepts: Risk is formally defined as the product of the probability of an event occurring during a time period of interest (POF) and the consequences associated with that event (COF).
Clause Requirement Detailed Guidance & Engineering Methodology Key Considerations & Scope Boundaries
4.1 What is Risk? Shall Risk is defined as the mathematical product of the probability of an event occurring during a specified timeframe and the magnitude of the consequences:

Risk = Probability of Failure (POF) × Consequence of Failure (COF)

In pressurized process equipment, failure is generally characterized by a loss of containment (leak, tear, rupture), leading to safety, environmental, or economic losses. 		Risk space is two-dimensional. An item with very low POF but catastrophic COF can possess equivalent or greater risk than an item with high POF and negligible COF.
4.2 Risk Management & Reduction Should Distinguishes between Risk Assessment (the analytical process of determining risk) and Risk Management (taking tangible actions to control or reduce risk).
  • Inspection reduces uncertainty in the degradation state, thereby managing POF.
  • Mitigation methods (isolation, blowdown, deluge, containment) manage COF.
  • Residual Risk: The baseline risk that remains after all feasible mitigation is implemented. Zero risk is technically and economically unachievable.
 Risk management decisions must balance risk reduction against economic and operational practicality (ALARP principle).
4.3 Relative vs Absolute Risk May Relative Risk: Compares equipment items against one another within a plant population (e.g. equipment ranked 1 through 500). Most owner-operators utilize relative risk ranking for inspection prioritization.

Absolute Risk: Computes the discrete, numeric risk value (e.g., $1.2 \times 10^{-5}$ fatalities/year or $45,000/year expected loss). Used when comparing plant risk against corporate risk criteria or societal acceptance limits. 		Relative risk assessments require fewer absolute data inputs, whereas absolute risk models require rigorous failure rate databases and validated consequence modeling.

Section 5

Introduction to Risk-Based Inspection

API RP 580
▼
Evolution of Mechanical Integrity: Section 5 contrasts historical calendar-based inspection against modern risk-informed strategies, establishes the continuum of RBI methods, and illustrates how inspection reduces uncertainty to control risk.
Clause Requirement Detailed Guidance & Engineering Methodology Key Considerations & Scope Boundaries
5.1 Evolution of Inspection Intervals Should Traces the transition of inspection planning across three distinct eras:
  1. Prescriptive / Time-Based: Fixed calendar intervals (e.g., internal inspection every 5 or 10 years regardless of condition).
  2. Condition-Based: Half-remaining-life calculations ($Interval = \text{Remaining Life} / 2$). Useful for general thinning but ineffective for localized cracking or sudden failure modes.
  3. Risk-Based (RBI): Dynamic intervals established by evaluating both probability (damage rate, susceptibility, inspection effectiveness) and consequence (toxic, flammable, financial).
 RBI enables plants to move inspection resources from low-risk equipment to high-risk circuits without sacrificing safety.
5.2 COF and POF for RBI Shall Inspection Primarily Impacts POF: Inspection does NOT alter the chemical hazards, pressure, or volume stored (COF). Rather, inspection reduces uncertainty regarding the current physical condition and active damage rates, thereby controlling and reducing POF.

COF remains relatively static unless physical engineering changes (inventory reduction, isolation valves, blowdown) are enacted. 		Programs that attempt to reduce consequence scores through inspection alone violate the fundamental physical principles of RBI.
5.3 Precision vs Accuracy Key for Success Differentiates between:
  • Accuracy: How close the estimated risk is to the true, actual risk. (Reflects correct damage mechanism identification, representative corrosion rates, and valid fluid properties).
  • Precision: The repeatability and granularity of the calculation (e.g. reporting risk to 5 decimal places or using a 5x5 vs 3x3 matrix).
High precision without accuracy creates a dangerous illusion of safety (false confidence). 		Refer to Figure 2 (dartboard analogy) and Table 1 for input variables influencing assessment accuracy.
5.4 Types of RBI Assessment Shall Defines the continuum of RBI methods (see Figure 3):
  • 5.4.1 General Continuum: Spectrum from purely qualitative to fully quantitative, with semi-quantitative methods occupying the continuum between.
  • 5.4.2 Qualitative RBI: Categorical scoring (Low/Medium/High) utilizing expert judgment and standardized decision trees. Fast, cost-effective, ideal for plant-wide screening.
  • 5.4.3 Semi-Quantitative RBI: Combines qualitative categorization with structured numerical algorithms (e.g., scoring rules, weighted factors, lookup matrices).
  • 5.4.4 Quantitative RBI: Rigorous numerical modeling utilizing statistical failure frequencies, detailed damage factors, event trees, and computerized dispersion physics (e.g., API RP 581).
 Continuum Principle: These methods are complementary rather than competing. A plant can screen 5,000 circuits qualitatively, and then model the top 200 high-risk vessels quantitatively.
5.5 Expected Outcomes of RBI Shall A valid RBI assessment shall generate three tangible engineering outcomes:
  1. Risk-Ranked Asset Registry: Complete ranking of all evaluated systems from highest to lowest risk.
  2. Targeted Inspection Plans: Specific scopes defining What damage mechanism to look for, Where to inspect (susceptible locations), How to inspect (NDE method & coverage), and When (due date).
  3. Risk Mitigation Recommendations: Non-inspection actions (metallurgy upgrade, IOW limits, relief design) for equipment where inspection is ineffective.
 An RBI assessment that only produces a risk ranking without actionable inspection plans is incomplete and non-compliant with API RP 580.
5.6 Using RBI for Risk Management Should Demonstrates how inspection mitigates risk over time (see Figure 4). Over time, without inspection, uncertainty accumulates and risk escalates. Performing an effective inspection resets the uncertainty and brings the calculated risk back below the acceptable threshold. If damage is progressing too rapidly, inspection alone cannot keep risk below the threshold; engineering repair or replacement becomes mandatory.
5.7 Continuous Improvement Tool Should Operates as a living closed-loop cycle:
Plan (develop RBI inspection plan) → Do (execute NDE inspection) → Check (evaluate as-found findings against predictions) → Act (update RBI model and adjust future intervals).		 Feeding as-found inspection findings back into the RBI database is mandatory to sustain model accuracy over the asset life cycle.
5.8 Relationship with Safety Initiatives Should Detailed interfaces with existing plant programs:
  • 5.8.1 Process Safety Management (PSM): Fulfills mechanical integrity requirements for RAGAGEP (OSHA 29 CFR 1910.119).
  • 5.8.2 Process Hazards Analysis (PHA): Leverages hazard scenarios, toxic release consequences, and credit for safeguards.
  • 5.8.3 Reliability-Centered Maintenance (RCM): Coordinates fixed equipment RBI with rotating equipment reliability programs.
  • 5.8.4 Integrity Operating Windows (IOWs): Sets critical operating parameter limits to protect against accelerated damage rates.
  • 5.8.5 Fitness-For-Service (FFS): Provides quantitative engineering evaluations of identified flaws (API 579-1).
  • 5.8.6 Damage Mechanism Review (DMR): Establishes the prerequisite degradation catalog (API 571).
  • 5.8.7 Other Initiatives: LOPA, safety instrumented systems (SIS), and incident investigations (API 585).
 Avoid creating "silos." RBI must leverage PHA consequence data and feed high-risk mechanical integrity findings back to plant safety teams.
5.9 Jurisdictional Requirements Shall Legal Primacy: In all cases, jurisdictional regulations (local, state, national boiler and pressure vessel authorities, National Board NBIC) take precedence.

Where an RBI program justifies an inspection interval longer than the prescriptive jurisdictional maximum, formal jurisdictional variance or approval shall be obtained where required by local statute. 		Owner-operators must document how their RBI program satisfies or interfaces with jurisdictional inspection rules and audit requirements.

Section 6

Planning the RBI Program

API RP 580
▼
Program Governance & Architecture: A successful RBI implementation requires disciplined project planning, clear boundary definitions, screening protocols, and management alignment before running technical models.
Figure 5 — Risk-Based Inspection Analysis Process Flowchart
Figure 5 Engineering Significance: The master workflow of an API RP 580 assessment: Planning → Data Collection → Damage Mechanism Review (DMR) → POF Assessment → COF Assessment → Risk Determination → Risk Management with Inspection & Mitigations → Updating & Continuous Improvement.
Clause Requirement Detailed Guidance & Engineering Methodology Key Considerations & Scope Boundaries
6.1 Organizing Work Process Shall The owner-operator shall establish and document an RBI management system that defines:
  • 6.1.1 Essential Steps: Project planning, boundary definition, data collection, DMR, POF, COF, risk calculation, inspection plan generation, execution, and updating.
  • 6.1.2 Reassessment & Updating: Defined triggers for updating equipment records and revalidating units.
  • 6.1.3 Continuous Improvement: Closing the feedback loop between field inspection findings and RBI model adjustments.
 Ensures the RBI program is institutionalized rather than depending on a single individual or contractor.
6.2 Establishing Goals & Objectives Shall Must explicitly state program objectives prior to commencing analysis:
  • Safety & Environmental: Eliminate catastrophic loss of containment events.
  • Reliability & Availability: Prevent unscheduled unit shutdowns.
  • Turnaround Optimization: Identify which equipment requires intrusive internal opening vs external non-intrusive NDE.
  • Regulatory Compliance: Comply with API 510/570/653 and jurisdictional mandates.
 Conflicting goals (e.g. reducing maintenance spend while ignoring safety risk) must be resolved early with executive management.
6.3 Initial Screening Should Allows high-level screening of plants, process units, and equipment systems to prioritize where detailed RBI should be conducted first.
  • 6.3.1 Plant-Level Screening: Identifying high-consequence units across a multi-unit complex.
  • 6.3.2 Process Unit Screening: Screening systems based on fluid toxicity, flammability, high energy (temperature/pressure), and aggressive damage mechanisms.
 Prevents expending extensive engineering hours modeling benign, low-risk utility systems (e.g. ambient plant air, potable water) at the expense of high-hazard circuits.
6.4 Establish Operating Boundaries Shall 6.4.1 Physical Boundaries: Clearly demarcate battery limits on P&IDs and PFDs. Identify included vs excluded equipment (e.g. piping circuits down to 2-inch or 1-inch, relief valves, offsites).

6.4.2 Operating Boundaries: Define the process envelope. Must evaluate not only normal steady-state operations, but also startups, shutdowns, steaming, decontamination, and upsets.

6.4.3 Utility & Secondary Systems: Establish boundary limits for cooling water, steam, and purge systems. 		Critical Warning: Equipment often experiences its most aggressive corrosion or cracking during startup, shutdown, or steaming out (e.g., polythionic acid SCC, dew-point condensation).
6.5 Selecting Method of Assessment Should Match the assessment method (qualitative, semi-quantitative, quantitative) to:
  • The objective of the assessment (broad screening vs precise interval extension).
  • Availability and quality of historical inspection and corrosion data.
  • Complexity of process hazards and potential consequence severity.
  • Resource, timeline, and computational budget constraints.
 A tiered approach is recommended: Qualitative screening of the entire facility → Semi-quantitative ranking of units → Quantitative modeling of critical equipment.
6.6 Factors Influencing Resources Key for Success Realistic estimation of project resources depends on:
  • Facility age, drawing availability (P&IDs, isometrics, MSDs).
  • Degree of inspection data digitization (access to electronic CMMS/IDMS records vs paper files).
  • Availability of site operations, process, and corrosion specialists.
  • Experience and maturity of the RBI team.
 Underestimating data gathering time is the #1 reason RBI implementation projects face schedule delays.
6.7 Transitioning an RBI Program Shall When transitioning from traditional calendar-based inspection to RBI, or migrating between different RBI software systems:
  • Reconcile baseline inspection records and active corrosion rates.
  • Ensure overdue inspections from legacy programs are addressed or formally risk-assessed.
  • Formally review and approve the new RBI inspection plans before superseding previous schedules.
 Maintain a complete audit trail during system cutover to demonstrate regulatory compliance to jurisdictional authorities.
6.8 Keys for Success: Planning Key for Success
✓ Core Keys for Planning Success
  • Obtain authentic, cross-departmental management sponsorship (Operations, Maintenance, Inspection, Safety).
  • Explicitly define physical battery limits and process envelopes prior to kicking off data gathering.
  • Ensure the team comprises qualified, experienced professionals rather than relying solely on automated software outputs.
  • Establish realistic project schedules with dedicated time allocated for site personnel interviews.
 Avoid viewing RBI as an administrative software exercise; it is an active engineering risk management methodology.

Data and Information Collection for RBI Assessment

API RP 580
▼
Data Quality Principles: The integrity of an RBI assessment is strictly limited by the accuracy, completeness, and recency of its input data. "Garbage-in, garbage-out" is the single greatest hazard in quantitative risk modeling.
Clause Requirement Detailed Guidance & Engineering Methodology Key Considerations & Scope Boundaries
8.1 General Fundamentals Shall Data collection shall encompass all relevant engineering, operational, and inspection information needed to identify damage mechanisms, estimate damage rates, calculate POF, and model consequence magnitude. Data collection is often the most resource-intensive phase of an RBI implementation. Structured data templates prevent omission.
8.2 RBI Data Needs Shall Specifies distinct data needs based on assessment methodology:
  • 8.2.1 General Needs: Basic equipment inventory, process fluid hazards, and high-level operating conditions.
  • 8.2.2 Data Needs for Qualitative RBI: General materials of construction, presence of corrosive constituents, basic operating parameters, and qualitative inspection history.
  • 8.2.3 Data Needs for Quantitative RBI: Precise metallurgical specifications, continuous temperature/pressure logs, detailed fluid stream chemical assays, complete CML thickness histories, and exact equipment dimensions.
  • 8.2.4 Data Needs for Semi-Quantitative RBI: Intermediate data sets combining qualitative screening data with discrete numerical operational and inspection data.
 Ensure data resolution matches the chosen modeling method. Over-precision on low-quality data creates false confidence.
8.3 Data Quality & Validation Shall The owner-operator shall establish data quality verification procedures:
  • Validation: Cross-check nominal drawing thicknesses against baseline field measurements.
  • Handling Missing Data: Where critical data is absent (e.g. trace chloride levels or unknown metallurgy), conservative default assumptions shall be used.
  • Sensitivity Analysis: Test how variations in questionable inputs influence the final risk category.
 Document all assumptions explicitly. If an assumption significantly drives risk, prioritize field verification (e.g. PMI testing or lab stream sampling).
8.4 Codes and Standards Should Collect the original design and construction codes (e.g. ASME Section VIII, Div 1 vs Div 2, B31.3) and post-construction inspection codes (API 510, 570, 653) under which the assets were fabricated and maintained. Vintage equipment fabricated under older code editions may have higher allowable stresses or different weld joint efficiencies.
8.5 Site-Specific Data Sources Key for Success Primary sources include:
  • Piping and Instrumentation Diagrams (P&IDs) and Process Flow Diagrams (PFDs).
  • Materials Selection Diagrams (MSDs) and Corrosion Control Documents (CCDs).
  • Inspection Data Management Systems (IDMS) and CMMS equipment files.
  • MOC logbooks, turnaround summary reports, and incident investigation findings.
 Always verify "as-built" and "as-operated" conditions. Facilities frequently modify piping configurations without updating master P&IDs.
8.6 Keys for Success: Data Key for Success
✓ Core Keys for Data Integrity
  • Never guess unknown metallurgy; verify with PMI (Positive Material Identification) if undocumented.
  • Do not rely solely on electronic databases; spot-check against original manufacturer data reports (U-1 forms).
  • Engage unit operators to uncover undocumented process deviations and seasonal excursions.
  • Document the basis for all engineering assumptions and default values used.
 Inaccurate input data invalidates the entire risk ranking, regardless of software sophistication.
Section 9

Damage Mechanism Review (DMR)

API RP 580
▼
The Technical Foundation of POF: A formal Damage Mechanism Review (DMR) is the mandatory prerequisite for assessing Probability of Failure. If a damage mechanism is not identified during the DMR, no inspection plan will be designed to detect it, and failure remains unmanaged.
Clause Requirement Detailed Guidance & Engineering Methodology Key Considerations & Scope Boundaries
9.1 General DMR Role Shall A comprehensive DMR shall be conducted for all equipment items and piping circuits included in the RBI assessment. Led by a qualified materials/corrosion specialist, it identifies:
  • All credible active and potential damage mechanisms (internal and external).
  • The expected deterioration rate (corrosion rate in mpy or mm/year).
  • The physical damage mode (thinning, localized pitting, cracking, blistering).
  • Specific susceptible equipment locations (deadlegs, injection points, vapor-liquid interfaces, weld heat-affected zones).
 DMR must refer directly to the comprehensive mechanism catalog in API RP 571.
9.2 Identifying Mechanisms & Rates Shall Systematically screens equipment against recognized industry degradation categories:
  • General & Localized Thinning: Sulfidation, naphthenic acid, ammonium bisulfide/chloride, hydrochloric acid, carbonic acid, erosion-corrosion.
  • Environmental Cracking: Wet H2S cracking (SSC, HIC, SOHIC), polythionic acid SCC, caustic cracking, amine SCC, chloride SCC.
  • Metallurgical & High-Temp: High-temperature hydrogen attack (HTHA), temper embrittlement, creep, graphitization, thermal fatigue.
  • External Degradation: Corrosion under insulation (CUI), atmospheric corrosion, soil corrosion.
 Determine both long-term corrosion rates (life of equipment) and short-term rates (recent operating period) to identify recent corrosion acceleration.
9.3 DMR Outcome Documentation Shall The outcome of the DMR shall be formally documented in a Corrosion Control Document (CCD) or DMR registry that specifies:
  1. Credible mechanisms per equipment item/circuit.
  2. Credible failure modes (e.g. pinhole leak for localized pitting vs sudden rupture for brittle fracture/HTHA).
  3. Estimated deterioration rate and basis for rate estimation (corrosion models, lab data, field UT data).
  4. Targeted inspection techniques capable of detecting each mechanism.
 DMR documentation serves as the technical baseline for developing the inspection plan and justifying interval decisions.
9.4 DMR Relationship with IOWs Should Integrates with Integrity Operating Windows (API 584). The corrosion rates established during the DMR are valid only while operations remain within defined process limits (temperature, velocity, inhibitor dosing, contaminant concentration).

When an IOW critical limit is breached, the DMR must be re-evaluated to determine if accelerated corrosion or cracking has occurred. 		RBI and IOWs form a coupled system: RBI assumes IOW compliance; IOWs monitor parameters that invalidate RBI assumptions.
9.5 Keys for Success: DMR Key for Success
✓ Core Keys for DMR Success
  • Ensure the materials/corrosion specialist leads the review; do not delegate DMR to junior modelers.
  • Examine transient conditions: wash water failures, desalter upsets, condensation during startups.
  • Clearly distinguish between thinning mechanisms (which can be monitored with UT thickness) and cracking mechanisms (which require WFMT, shear-wave UT, or PAUT).
  • Identify injection points and mix points as dedicated high-risk inspection locations.
 The most severe process safety incidents occur from unrecognized damage mechanisms, not from miscalculating corrosion rates by a few percent.
Section 10

Assessing Probability of Failure (POF)

API RP 580
▼
Quantifying Equipment Likelihood of Failure: POF evaluates the likelihood that an equipment item will lose containment under operating conditions. POF accounts for active damage mechanisms, expected corrosion rates, elapsed operating time, effectiveness of past inspections, and management systems.
Clause Requirement Detailed Guidance & Engineering Methodology Key Considerations & Scope Boundaries
10.1 Introduction to POF Shall POF analysis shall address all credible damage mechanisms identified during the DMR. POF is dynamic and time-dependent: as an asset remains in service, cumulative damage increases, causing POF to rise unless mitigated by inspection or repair. Focuses on pressure boundary loss of containment. Functional failures (e.g. valve sticking, tray collapse) are addressed separately or through RCM.
10.2 Units of Measure in POF Should Units depend on the chosen RBI approach:
  • Qualitative: Categorical rankings (e.g., Low, Medium, High; or Categories 1 through 5/6).
  • Quantitative: Annual failure probability expressed as failure frequency (failures or events per year, e.g., $10^{-4}$ events/year).
 Category ranges must be clearly defined in the owner-operator's RBI manual to avoid ambiguous interpretations between different assessors.
10.3 Types of POF Analysis Should
  • 10.3.1 General Continuum: Spectrum from qualitative ranking to quantitative probabilistic calculations.
  • 10.3.2 Qualitative POF: Evaluates units, materials, and corrosiveness using expert judgment, scoring rules, and decision trees. Quick and effective for ranking.
  • 10.3.3 Quantitative POF: Typically combines three core components (as implemented in API 581):

    POF(t) = gff × D_f(t) × F_MS

    Where:
    • gff: Generic Failure Frequency (industry baseline failure rate by equipment type).
    • D_f(t): Damage Factor (scales gff based on specific damage mechanisms, rates, and past inspection effectiveness).
    • F_MS: Management Systems Factor (reflects facility safety culture and PSM adherence).
 Ensure the chosen method allows sensitivity analysis to verify that calculated POF is realistic and conservative.
10.4 Determination of POF Shall The determination of POF shall systematically evaluate:
  1. 10.4.2 Failure Mode Identification: Distinguishing between pinholes, localized leaks, and full-bore ruptures.
  2. 10.4.3 Credible Damage Mechanisms: Internal thinning, external CUI, cracking mechanisms, and high-temp degradation.
  3. 10.4.4 Damage Rates & Susceptibility: Measuring remaining corrosion allowance ($t_{actual} - t_{min}$) and assessing crack propagation probability.
  4. 10.4.5 Inspection Effectiveness: Past inspection methods, coverage, and detection capability. Credit is awarded only for inspections specifically tailored to the active mechanism.
 Performing general external visual inspection provides zero credit against internal thinning or cracking mechanisms.
10.5 Keys for Success: POF Key for Success
✓ Core Keys for POF Accuracy
  • Always base POF on the specific active damage mechanism, not generic corrosion rates.
  • Incorporate inspection effectiveness credits rigorously; do not inflate confidence without verified NDE reports.
  • Account for localized damage (pitting, CUI, erosion) using specialized statistical models rather than general wall loss formulas.
  • Document the qualified personnel authorized to modify generic failure frequencies.
 Failing to account for localized damage acceleration is a primary cause of unexpected loss of containment.

Assessing Consequence of Failure (COF)

API RP 580
▼
The Magnitude of Loss: Consequence of Failure evaluates the potential outcomes resulting from a loss of containment. It quantifies hazards across safety, health, environmental, and financial dimensions based on fluid properties, inventory, release rate, and mitigation safeguards.
Figure 6 — Determination of Consequence of Failure (COF) Flowchart
Figure 6 Engineering Significance: The formal COF determination sequence: Fluid characterization → Inventory determination → Release rate modeling → Post-release state & phase calculation → Event tree consequence outcome (Fire, Explosion, Toxic, Environmental, Financial) → Consequence category assignment.
Clause Requirement Detailed Guidance & Engineering Methodology Key Considerations & Scope Boundaries
11.1 Introduction to COF Shall COF analysis shall address the consequences resulting from loss of containment (11.1.1 General). Focuses primarily on pressure boundary leaks, tears, and ruptures, but may also evaluate other functional failures (11.1.2):
  • Heat exchanger tube failures (shell overpressurization or cooling water contamination).
  • Pressure-relief device failures (failure to open or leakage).
  • Rotating equipment mechanical failures (pump seal blowouts).
 COF is primarily governed by process fluid hazards and available inventory, not by inspection frequency.
11.2 Types of COF Analysis Should
  • 11.2.2 Qualitative COF Analysis: Bins equipment into consequence categories (e.g. Low, Medium, High; or Categories A through E) based on operating temperature, pressure, toxicity ratings (NFPA), and approximate inventory.
  • 11.2.3 Semi-Quantitative COF: Categorizes consequences using structured scoring matrices combining fluid hazard ratings with estimated inventory buckets.
  • 11.2.4 Quantitative COF: Simulates fluid release thermodynamics, discharge rates, aerosol formation, atmospheric dispersion (Gaussian/dense gas), pool fires, jet fires, vapor cloud explosions (VCE), and financial calculations.
 Qualitative methods are suitable for initial screening; quantitative modeling is warranted when distinguishing between high-consequence units.
11.3 Units of Measure in COF Shall COF must be expressed in defined, consistent metrics across all categories:
  • 11.3.2 Safety & Health Consequences: Thresholds for first-aid, medical treatment, lost-time injury, permanent disability, or fatalities.
  • 11.3.3 Financial / Economic Consequences: Total monetary loss ($) combining equipment repair, product loss, and business downtime.
  • 11.3.4 Affected Area ($ft^2$ or $m^2$): Surface area experiencing thermal radiation (>5 kW/m²), blast overpressure (>1 psi), or toxic concentration (>ERPG-2).
  • 11.3.5 Environmental Damage: Acres of land affected, miles of shoreline impacted, barrels spilled, restoration cost ($).
  • 11.3.6 Categorizing SHE Consequence: Binning SHE impacts into standardized pre-defined qualitative categories (Tables 7 & 8).
 Owner-operators must clearly document whether safety consequence or financial consequence drives the primary risk matrix.
11.4 Volume of Fluid Released Shall The release volume calculation shall consider:
  • Available Volume: Fluid contained between isolation valves that can be rapidly closed.
  • Hole Size Class: Small leak (0.25"), medium (1"), large (4"), or catastrophic full-bore rupture.
  • Isolation Capability: Credit for remotely operated shutoff valves (ROSOVs) vs manual block valves. (Manual valves in fire zones cannot be credited for quick isolation).
  • De-inventory Systems: Credit for emergency depressuring to flare (blowdown).
 Detection and isolation systems significantly reduce the release duration, cutting flammable and toxic consequence areas substantially.
11.5 Consequence Effect Categories Should Evaluates five distinct hazard categories:
  1. 11.5.1 Flammable & Explosive: Pool fires, jet fires, fireballs, flash fires, vapor cloud explosions (VCE), BLEVEs.
  2. 11.5.2 Toxic Releases: Dispersion of H2S, HF acid, chlorine, ammonia, benzene exceeding permissible exposure thresholds.
  3. 11.5.3 Non-Flammable / Non-Toxic: Physical blast overpressure from high-pressure gas/steam, thermal burns (steam, hot water), cryogenic exposure.
  4. 11.5.4 Environmental Impact: Soil, groundwater, or surface water contamination requiring regulatory notification and remediation.
  5. 11.5.5 Commercial Impact: Financial cost of unit shutdown, lost product sales, and equipment rebuild.
 In high-margin units, production loss consequence often dwarfs physical equipment damage cost by an order of magnitude.
11.6 Determination of COF Shall The final COF category or numerical value shall follow the structured sequence shown in Figure 6:
  • Characterize fluid stream composition and physical properties.
  • Determine total available inventory.
  • Calculate discharge rate and total mass released.
  • Determine post-release fluid phase (liquid, vapor, two-phase aerosol).
  • Model dispersion, thermal radiation, and overpressure footprints.
  • Assign final COF category or dollar loss value.
 Document the basis for all consequence category cutoff limits.
11.7 Keys for Success: COF Key for Success
✓ Core Keys for COF Accuracy
  • Verify available inventory on P&IDs; account for un-isolated connected vessels and surge capacity.
  • Do not assume manual block valves can be closed during an active toxic cloud or pool fire.
  • Include business interruption duration; equipment replacement lead times (forges, reactors) can exceed 12-24 months.
  • Coordinate with plant PHA/PSM teams to ensure consequence ratings are consistent across the facility.
 Underestimating consequence potential leads to under-classifying critical assets, creating high risk blindness.

Section 12

Risk Determination, Assessment, and Management

API RP 580
▼
The Synthesis of Risk: Risk Determination brings together POF and COF to calculate total asset risk, position equipment on the risk matrix or iso-risk plot, evaluate acceptability against corporate criteria (ALARP), and conduct sensitivity analyses.
Clause Requirement Detailed Guidance & Engineering Methodology Key Considerations & Scope Boundaries
12.1 Purpose of Risk Determination Shall Combines the results of the POF assessment and COF assessment to establish an integrated risk ranking. The objective is to identify assets requiring active risk management and prioritize inspection resources accordingly. Establishes the foundation for all inspection interval, scope, and technique decisions.
12.2 Determination of Risk Shall Risk is determined through:
  • Matrix Intersection: Plotting the POF category (1 to 5) and COF category (A to E) on a standard risk matrix (see Figure 7).
  • 12.2.1 Probability of a Specific Consequence: Evaluating conditional probabilities of specific release outcomes (e.g. ignition vs unignited dispersion).
  • 12.2.2 Event Tree Approach: Tracing release ignition probabilities, explosion likelihoods, and weather wind roses.
 Ensure that multi-mechanism assets combine the probabilities of each credible mechanism rather than simply picking the single worst mechanism.
12.3 Acceptable Levels of Risk Shall The owner-operator shall formally establish and document corporate Risk Criteria and acceptable risk boundaries:
  • Unacceptable Region (High Risk): Operation not permitted without immediate inspection or mitigation.
  • ALARP Region (Medium Risk): Risk is tolerated only if further risk reduction is demonstrated to be economically or technically impracticable.
  • Acceptable Region (Low Risk): Risk is broadly acceptable; standard routine monitoring applies.
 Risk criteria must be approved by senior executive facility management, not by the inspection contractor alone.
12.4 Sensitivity Analysis Should Perform sensitivity analysis by varying critical input variables (corrosion rate, operating temperature, toxic concentration, isolation time) to observe their effect on final risk ranking.

Identifies the risk drivers and tests whether conclusions depend heavily on uncertain assumptions. 		If a minor 10% change in temperature causes an asset to jump from Low to High risk, that operating variable must be governed by an IOW (API 584).
12.5 Assumptions Management Shall All assumptions used in the determination of POF, COF, and risk shall be documented in the permanent RBI project file. Assumptions must be periodically reviewed and verified against field operating data. Undocumented assumptions create major legal and audit vulnerabilities during regulatory mechanical integrity audits.
12.6 Risk Presentation Shall Risk shall be clearly presented to stakeholders using standardized formats:
  • Risk Matrix (Figure 7): 5x5 or 3x3 grid displaying POF categories vs COF categories with color-coded risk levels (Red = High, Yellow = Medium, Green = Low).
  • Quantitative Risk Plot (Figure 8): Continuous log-log graph of failure frequency vs consequence with iso-risk threshold lines.
 Risk presentation must facilitate rapid decision-making for operations, maintenance, and turnaround planning teams.
12.7 Risk Thresholds Shall Define clear risk threshold lines on the matrix or plot. The point in time when an asset's projected risk crosses above the risk threshold dictates the maximum allowable inspection due date. Exceeding the risk threshold without performing an inspection or formal mitigation constitutes an overdue inspection condition.
12.8 Keys for Success: Determination Key for Success
✓ Core Keys for Risk Determination Success
  • Calibrate risk criteria to company-wide corporate risk policies (do not create an isolated inspection risk matrix).
  • Involve plant leadership in reviewing the final risk-ranked asset distribution.
  • Use sensitivity analysis to validate questionable corrosion rates and fluid assumptions.
  • Focus risk management actions on the top 10-20% highest-risk assets where 80% of total plant risk resides.
 Pareto Principle in RBI: Typically, ~80% of total plant risk resides in only ~10-20% of the equipment items.

Section 13

Risk Management with Inspection Activities

API RP 580
▼
The Primary Driver of Risk Reduction: Inspection is the primary operational tool used to control Probability of Failure. Section 13 establishes the methodology for converting risk scores into actionable, highly targeted inspection plans (technique, coverage, timing) and managing inspection effectiveness.
Clause Requirement Detailed Guidance & Engineering Methodology Key Considerations & Scope Boundaries
13.1 Risk Management Overview Shall The ultimate objective of an RBI program is not ranking equipment, but executing targeted inspection activities that actively manage and reduce asset risk below acceptable thresholds. A risk assessment without an actionable, implemented inspection plan provides zero risk reduction value.
13.2 Reducing Uncertainty via Inspection Shall Inspection manages risk by reducing uncertainty regarding:
  • Active damage mechanisms present.
  • Actual remaining wall thickness and deterioration rate.
  • Presence, depth, and propagation rate of cracks or localized flaws.
Inspection Effectiveness Categories (13.2.2):
  • Category A (Highly Effective): Detects 90-100% of damage; accurately quantifies morphology and depth (e.g. 100% automated ultrasonic scanning, profile radiography of all fittings).
  • Category B (Usually Effective): Detects 70-90% of damage; good quantification (e.g. extensive grid UT, selective profile RT).
  • Category C (Fairly Effective): Detects 50-70% of damage; detects presence but limited quantification (e.g. spot UT thickness at TMLs).
  • Category D (Poorly Effective): Detects <50% of damage; low probability of detection (e.g. spot visual external exam for internal thinning).
  • Category E (Ineffective): Zero capability to detect the active damage mechanism.
 Credit can only be awarded if the NDE technique is physically capable of detecting the specific damage mode (e.g. UT thickness is Category E for wet H2S cracking).
13.3 Identifying Opportunities Should Use RBI results to identify:
  • High Yield Inspections: Equipment where an effective inspection significantly drops POF and risk.
  • Diminishing Returns: Equipment where performing additional inspections yields negligible risk reduction (e.g. low consequence vessels or noble alloy circuits).
  • Non-Inspectable Situations: Equipment where inspection cannot reduce risk (e.g., rapid environmental cracking, brittle fracture, or uninspectable geometries). Here, non-inspection mitigations are required.
 Avoid over-inspecting low-risk assets while high-risk circuits receive superficial examinations.
13.4 Establishing Inspection Strategy Shall The inspection plan shall explicitly specify four parameters:
  1. What: The specific active and potential damage mechanisms targeted.
  2. Where: Exact susceptible physical locations (injection points, deadlegs, CUI-prone areas, weld HAZ, vapor-liquid interfaces).
  3. How: The designated NDE method, surface preparation, probe selection, and examination coverage extent (% surface or number of points).
  4. When: The inspection due date, established when projected risk reaches the corporate risk threshold.
 Inspection plans must be communicated to the field inspector with clear acceptance/rejection criteria and reporting instructions.
13.5 Managing Inspection Costs Should RBI enables significant cost optimization by:
  • Eliminating unnecessary internal vessel entries (confined space entry) in favor of non-intrusive external NDE (on-stream UT/profile RT).
  • Extending inspection intervals on low-risk equipment within code-allowable limits (API 510/570/653).
  • Leveling turnaround (TAR) workloads and focusing contractor budgets on high-risk critical path items.
 Cost reductions must be a byproduct of sound risk management, not the primary objective compromising safety.
13.6 Assessing Inspection Results Shall Following execution of an inspection, the as-found findings shall be assessed by the inspector and materials specialist:
  • Compare measured thickness and damage depth against RBI model predictions.
  • Calculate updated short-term and long-term corrosion rates.
  • Verify if new, unexpected damage mechanisms were discovered.
  • Feed verified data back into the RBI software to calculate the new risk baseline and future due date.
 Closing the feedback loop is mandatory. If as-found corrosion rates exceed predictions, the inspection interval must be shortened immediately.
13.7 Life Cycle Cost Optimization Should RBI supports life cycle management: evaluating whether repeated high-frequency inspections are more costly than metallurgy upgrades (e.g. upgrading carbon steel to stainless steel or alloy cladding) or equipment replacement. Enables maintenance and reliability engineers to present justified capital project business cases.
13.8 Modifying Plans After Failure Shall If a loss of containment, leak, or near-miss occurs in the plant:
  • Determine the root cause damage mechanism.
  • Immediately evaluate why the existing RBI model failed to predict the event (e.g. unmodeled mechanism, missing data, incorrect IOW assumption).
  • Re-assess all sibling equipment, parallel piping circuits, and similar process services across the facility.
  • Update inspection plans and due dates accordingly.
 Regulatory authorities (OSHA, EPA, jurisdictional boards) specifically audit post-failure RBI model updates during incident investigations.
13.9 Keys for Success: Inspection Key for Success
✓ Core Keys for Inspection Strategy Success
  • Match NDE techniques directly to damage mechanisms (e.g., use shear-wave UT/PAUT for cracks, profile RT for small-bore piping).
  • Focus inspections on high-susceptibility zones rather than random generic thickness readings.
  • Ensure inspectors understand the specific damage mechanism they are tasked with finding.
  • Mandate timely post-inspection data entry into the IDMS/RBI system to maintain evergreen models.
 An ineffective inspection technique creates dangerous false confidence and leaves risk completely unmitigated.
Section 14

Other Risk Management Methods

API RP 580
▼
Beyond Inspection: When inspection cannot reduce risk to acceptable levels (e.g. uninspectable equipment, rapid catastrophic mechanisms, or overwhelming consequence potential), alternative engineering and operational risk management methods must be deployed.
Clause Requirement Detailed Guidance & Engineering Methodology Key Considerations & Scope Boundaries
14.1 General Scope Shall When inspection is ineffective, impractical, or economically inferior, the owner-operator shall evaluate and implement non-inspection risk mitigation measures to reduce POF, COF, or both. Inspection is only one tool in the mechanical integrity toolbox. High-consequence assets often require engineered hardware barriers.
14.2 Replacement & Repair Should
  • Equipment Repair: Weld overlays, flush patches, strip lining, or composite wraps executed in accordance with API 510/570.
  • Metallurgy Upgrade: Replacing carbon steel with corrosion-resistant alloys (CRAs), clad plates, or lined vessels to eliminate degradation susceptibility.
 Upgrading metallurgy permanently resets the damage factor ($D_f$) and eliminates recurring inspection overhead.
14.3 Fitness-for-Service (FFS) Should Apply API 579-1 / ASME FFS-1 quantitative flaw evaluations when inspection detects localized wall thinning, pitting, blisters, or crack-like indications:
  • Level 1: Screening evaluation by plant inspector using simplified handbook charts.
  • Level 2: Engineering evaluation by mechanical engineer with detailed stress calculations.
  • Level 3: Advanced numerical modeling (finite element analysis FEA) and fracture mechanics.
 FFS calculations provide the basis for safe continued operation, revised retirement thicknesses, or required de-rating.
14.4 Equipment Modification & Re-rating May Modify operating parameters or re-rate equipment:
  • De-rating: Lowering maximum allowable working pressure (MAWP) or maximum design temperature to restore safety margins without physical replacement.
  • Re-design: Altering piping geometry to eliminate turbulence, replacing restrictive elbows with long-radius bends, or removing deadlegs.
 De-rating requires updating vessel nameplates and re-calculating relief valve capacities.
14.5 Emergency Isolation (ROSOVs) Should Install remotely operated shutoff valves (ROSOVs) or automated emergency shutdown valves (ESDVs) to rapidly isolate leaking equipment from large upstream and downstream inventories. Directly cuts the release duration and total mass spilled, drastically shrinking flammable and toxic consequence areas.
14.6 Emergency Depressuring (Blowdown) Should Provide automated or remote-actuated emergency depressuring systems routed to closed flare or scrubber headers. Rapidly dumps pressure, reducing driving force for loss of containment and preventing BLEVEs during exposure fires. Must verify that downstream flare systems have sufficient hydraulic capacity to handle peak blowdown rates.
14.7 Process Modification Should Adjust chemical environment to reduce corrosion rates:
  • Injecting corrosion inhibitors, neutralizing amines, or oxygen scavengers.
  • Desalting optimization to remove crude oil chlorides.
  • Operating temperature reduction to drop below dew-point acid condensation or HTHA Nelson curves.
 Process modifications must be locked in via formal Integrity Operating Windows (IOWs) with DCS alarm thresholds.
14.8 Reduce Inventory May Reduce the standing volume of hazardous fluids:
  • Operating surge vessels and columns at lower liquid levels.
  • Replacing large bulk storage tanks with smaller buffer vessels or just-in-time chemical delivery.
 Directly reduces the available inventory ($COF$) in quantitative dispersion modeling.
14.9 Water Spray & Deluge Should Install fixed water deluge systems over vessels containing pressurized light hydrocarbons: provides structural cooling to prevent vessel wall overheating and catastrophic thermal rupture during an external fire. Deluge systems must be regularly flow-tested and maintained to prevent plugged spray nozzles.
14.10 Water Curtains May Deploy perimeter water curtains or steam curtains to absorb, knock down, or dilute water-soluble toxic vapors (e.g. HF acid, ammonia, chlorine) or disperse heavy flammable vapor clouds. Highly effective consequence mitigation barrier for toxic alkylation and chemical synthesis units.
14.11 Blast-Resistant Construction Should Strengthen control rooms, operator shelters, and critical sub-stations against vapor cloud explosion (VCE) blast overpressure in accordance with API RP 752 / 753. Protects personnel population from building collapse, dramatically reducing safety consequence metrics.
14.12 Additional Methods May Install secondary containment dikes, vapor barrier walls, inert gas purging, or specialized leak detection sensors (optical flame detectors, acoustic ultrasonic leak sensors). Layering multiple independent protection layers (LOPA) ensures defense-in-depth.
14.13 Keys for Success: Mitigations Key for Success
✓ Core Keys for Non-Inspection Mitigations
  • Do not treat inspection as the default answer for every risk; recognize when engineering redesign is necessary.
  • Formally verify that credited mitigation barriers (ROSOVs, deluge, blowdown) are fully functional and periodically tested.
  • Ensure process modifications are backed by MOC and monitored through real-time IOW dashboard alarms.
  • Integrate FFS evaluations directly into the RBI database to establish accurate revised baseline life.
 Crediting a safeguard that fails to operate in an emergency creates catastrophic unmitigated consequence exposure.

The Evergreen Process: An RBI assessment is never a static, one-time study; it is an evergreen management process. Section 15 defines the distinct roles of routine assessment updates versus comprehensive formal revalidations, along with mandatory event-driven triggers.
Figure 9 — Relationship of Updates and Revalidations
Figure 9 Engineering Significance: Depicts the ongoing lifecycle relationship between frequent, incremental Updates (routine as-found inspection entry, MOC changes, turnaround work scopes) and periodic comprehensive Revalidations (full-scale multi-disciplinary team reviews every 5 to 10 years or turnaround cycles).
Clause Requirement Detailed Guidance & Engineering Methodology Key Considerations & Scope Boundaries
15.1 In General & Evergreen Mandate Shall RBI assessments shall be updated periodically and following significant plant events. Operating an RBI program with outdated models or stale data violates API 580 and post-construction inspection codes (API 510/570/653). An unmaintained RBI model quickly diverges from actual physical plant reality, creating blind spots.
15.2 RBI Assessment Updates Shall Updates: Routine, targeted adjustments made to specific equipment items or circuits:
  • a) Updating inspection results after an inspection is completed, including changes to next planned inspection date and/or task.
  • b) New information made available regarding process or equipment changes (IOW limits exceeded, MOC, PHA).
  • c) Updating the RBI database before a turnaround (or shutdown) to establish new risk rankings and update inspection plans.
  • d) Repairs, alterations, or replacements of equipment or components.
  • e) After a turnaround (or shutdown) where inspections or equipment modifications occurred.
 Updates are completed as needed by the risk analyst supported by other SMEs to keep current with plant events.
15.3 RBI Revalidations Shall Revalidations (15.3.1 & 15.3.2): Comprehensive, formal re-evaluations of the entire unit, area, system, or site RBI program by the full multidisciplinary team:
  • Re-evaluates all damage mechanisms (DMR) against cumulative operating history.
  • Reviews process stream compositions, crude slate shifts, and chemical feed changes.
  • Validates software models, consequence thresholds, and corporate risk criteria.
  • Re-benchmarks risk distributions and updates the master 5-to-10 year inspection strategy.
Frequency: Typically conducted every 5 to 10 years, or aligned with governing inspection code limits (e.g. 10-year maximum). 		Revalidation requires formal multidisciplinary team participation similar to the initial baseline assessment.
15.4 Other Triggering Events Shall The owner-operator shall establish management system triggers that mandate unscheduled RBI updates:
  • 15.4.1 Management of Change (MOC): Physical or process modifications affecting damage rates or fluid hazards.
  • 15.4.2 Process Changes & Feedstock Variations: Feedstock changes (e.g. high acid or opportunity crudes).
  • 15.4.3 Operating Excursions & IOW Violations: Time-independent process condition exceedances.
  • 15.4.4 Unexpected Inspection Findings: Accelerated thinning, unexpected cracking, or damage in unanticipated locations.
  • 15.4.5 Before & After Maintenance Turnarounds: Planning scopes pre-TAR and recording as-found data post-TAR.
 Failing to update RBI after an aggressive feedstock change has caused numerous catastrophic refinery failures.
15.5 Keys for Success: Updating Key for Success
✓ Core Keys for Evergreen RBI Success
  • Integrate RBI directly into the plant MOC workflow; require an RBI review checkbox on all process and metallurgy MOCs.
  • Enforce mandatory data entry deadlines following turnaround completions (e.g. within 60-90 days post-startup).
  • Schedule revalidations well in advance of major turnarounds to drive the turnaround inspection scope.
  • Track and audit "overdue updates" with the same rigor as overdue physical inspections.
 An RBI model that is not updated ceases to be an asset integrity tool and becomes a compliance liability.

Section 16

RBI Documentation and Recordkeeping

API RP 580
▼
The Audit Trail: Documenting the entire RBI process is a mandatory legal and technical requirement. If an RBI decision or interval extension is not documented with clear engineering justification, it is legally and technically non-existent under regulatory and code scrutiny.
Clause Requirement Detailed Guidance & Engineering Methodology Key Considerations & Scope Boundaries
16.1 Documenting Management System Shall The owner-operator shall maintain a written RBI Program Manual that defines:
  • The specific RBI methodology, algorithms, and software used.
  • Software validation and quality control procedures.
  • Team roles, minimum qualification requirements, and training records.
  • Corporate risk criteria, matrix definitions, and acceptable risk thresholds.
  • Reassessment frequency and MOC trigger workflows.
 This manual is the primary document reviewed by jurisdictional inspectors and OSHA mechanical integrity auditors.
16.2 Documenting the Assessment Shall For every assessment performed, the permanent project file shall archive:
  • Physical and operating boundaries (marked-up P&IDs, battery limits).
  • Complete DMR report: active mechanisms, estimated deterioration rates, and basis for rates.
  • All input data used: design, operational, fluid properties, and historical inspection records.
  • All engineering assumptions and default values used for missing data.
  • Detailed POF, COF, and risk calculation results.
 Ensure documentation allows an independent third party to retrace every step of the calculation and reproduce identical risk results.
16.3 Documenting the Outcome Shall The final output deliverables shall be permanently recorded:
  • Risk-Ranked Asset Registry: Complete baseline and mitigated risk scores.
  • Actionable Inspection Plans: NDE methods, coverage, locations, and inspection due dates loaded into the plant CMMS/IDMS.
  • Mitigation Recommendations: Non-inspection actions (repairs, metallurgy upgrades, IOWs, emergency isolation) with assigned action owners and target completion dates.
  • Variance Documentation: Engineering justifications for interval extensions beyond prescriptive code intervals.
 Inspection plans must be traceable to work orders executed in the field.
16.4 Keys for Success: Records Key for Success
✓ Core Keys for Documentation Success
  • Archive marked-up P&IDs and PFDs showing exact circuit boundary breaks.
  • Store electronic backup of the raw RBI database and software input files for every formal assessment and revalidation.
  • Maintain signed authorization forms from the multidisciplinary team and plant management.
  • Ensure inspection work orders in CMMS clearly reference the RBI inspection plan ID.
 In regulatory audits, undocumented inspection interval extensions are cited as willful mechanical integrity violations.
